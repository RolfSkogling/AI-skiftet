CERT-SE warns of autonomous AI agents – Talos finds vote-driven malware

Cisco Talos, the threat intelligence unit of US networking giant Cisco, reported on 22 September on malware called Closedquorum, which Talos describes as the first publicly documented implant that hands its operating decisions…

Read the full itemCollapse

Cisco Talos, the threat intelligence unit of US networking giant Cisco, reported on 22 September on malware called Closedquorum, which Talos describes as the first publicly documented implant that hands its operating decisions entirely to AI models. The find comes just days after Sweden's national CSIRT, CERT-SE, warned in its weekly briefing that AI agents are increasingly being used to run cyberattacks with no human in the loop.

Closedquorum queries up to four commercial AI services – DeepSeek, Qwen, Mistral and Google's Gemini – and lets them vote on its next move: steal, inject, persist or move. Whichever action gets the most votes is carried out, and DeepSeek's vote breaks any tie. The code itself states, according to Talos: “the session is closed; no humans are admitted.” Talos simultaneously released Cairn, an open-source toolkit so others can track similar AI-driven malware.

CERT-SE's briefing also pointed to Anthropic's own report on threat actors misusing Claude, and noted that the EU's cybersecurity agency ENISA has been given access to Anthropic's Mythos 5 and OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra for security testing. Sweden's National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC) and AI Sweden are jointly hosting a seminar on 22 October on vulnerability management in an AI-driven threat landscape.

Source: Cisco Talos, The Hacker News, CERT-SE

US network and cloud company Akamai announced a seven-year, $11.6 billion agreement with AI firm Anthropic on 24 September to meet growing demand for computing capacity. The deal includes an option for a further $9 billion, and Akamai's share price rose roughly 20 percent on the news.

The global race for capacity therefore risks sharpening competition for grid connections in the Nordics as well.

One of Norway's largest banks, DNB, has banned employees and visitors from wearing AI glasses in its premises with immediate effect. Communications director Liselotte Lunde says the bank wants a workplace where no one has to wonder if colleagues or customers are being secretly filmed.

Quantum

Quantum firm IonQ shows real-time error correction on ordinary hardware

US quantum company IonQ said on September 23 that it had tested an end-to-end real-time quantum error decoder running on an ordinary standard processor, with no specialised hardware required.

Read the full itemCollapse

US quantum company IonQ said on September 23 that it had tested an end-to-end real-time quantum error decoder running on an ordinary standard processor, with no specialised hardware required. The system handled simulated circuits with up to 408 logical qubits and more than 31.5 million quantum operations.

The result, the company says, addresses a classic bottleneck: the classical computers that correct quantum errors in real time typically get overwhelmed, forcing the quantum system to pause. IonQ's solution handles the work in the background with only 0.02 percent extra computing overhead, according to the company. Research lead Nicolas Delfosse called the result ”an important milestone” on the road to quantum computers with thousands of qubits, far more than the 256 qubits IonQ's current system has. The stock rose about 12 percent on the news.

For the Nordics, error correction is the core question in the race for a usable quantum computer. Chalmers' Wallenberg Centre for Quantum Technology (WACQT) works on the same problem for its Swedish qubits, and Finland's IQM builds its own quantum computers. The US Department of Energy recently launched a 215 million dollar competition specifically for fault-tolerant quantum computers – a field where both American and Nordic research groups are now investing heavily.

Anthropic cuts the list price 20 percent on its new top model Claude Opus 5.5

Anthropic released Claude Opus 5.5 on 22 September and cut the list price by 20 percent per token at the same time: 4 dollars per million input tokens against 5 previously, and 20 dollars per million output tokens against 25.

Read the full itemCollapse

Anthropic released Claude Opus 5.5 on 22 September and cut the list price by 20 percent per token at the same time: 4 dollars per million input tokens against 5 previously, and 20 dollars per million output tokens against 25. It is the first model in the 5.5 family and arrives two months after Opus 5. Cache reads, which according to the company account for the bulk of the cost in agentic and coding work, fall from 50 to 20 cents per million tokens. The figure Anthropic itself highlights is 40 percent instead — but that applies to cost per task at default settings and is the company's own modelling, combining the lower token price with the claim that the model uses fewer tokens and generates output more than 30 percent faster.

The outward price comparison is less clear-cut than the inward cut. OpenAI's top model GPT-6 Astra costs 10 dollars in and 50 out per million tokens, while Google's Gemini 3.1 Pro sits at 2 and 12 for prompts up to 200 000 tokens. Opus 5.5 is therefore clearly cheaper than OpenAI's top model but still more expensive per token than Google's. The external pre-release testing was done by two parties, Frontier Design and METR, and METR has published its evaluation: it describes Opus 5.5 as an incremental improvement compared with Claude Fable 5.1 on METR's quantitative tests, not a discontinuous jump, with remaining weaknesses on tasks with a long time horizon. METR also notes that the review tested neither compliance with thresholds in Anthropic's own policies nor the model's alignment properties, and that Anthropic was given the opportunity to review and edit the text before publication. No published result from Frontier Design was found. The claim that Opus 5.5 is the strongest model the company has tested therefore refers to Anthropic's own automated behavioural audit across nearly 2 000 scenarios, and the figures on performance, speed, cost per task and alignment likewise come from Anthropic's own release announcement. It also quotes an early tester saying ”it writes the way I do”. Anthropic states that Sonnet 5.5 and Haiku 5.5 will follow ”in the coming weeks”, without giving a date.

For the Nordics: price is the part that bites here. A frontier model at 4 dollars per million input tokens moves the line for what a smaller team can afford to run in production — Nordic AI startups, municipalities and smaller universities have so far passed on the top models for cost reasons and run a cheaper one even where it was not good enough. At the same time Google is still cheaper per token, so the choice is not obvious. The model is available via AWS, Google Cloud and Microsoft Azure, the three cloud platforms Nordic large companies normally reach a frontier model through, and Anthropic states that it ships with watermarking to comply with the AI Act's labelling rules, whose transparency requirements began to apply on 2 August 2026. For KTH, Chalmers, NTNU and the University of Oslo the token price directly determines how much research computation fits inside a grant from Vinnova or the Research Council of Norway.

Source: Anthropic, METR, TechCrunch

Alphabet's robotics unit opens the core of its industrial platform under Apache 2.0

Intrinsic, Alphabet's industrial robotics software company, released Intrinsic Core as open source under an Apache 2.0 licence at ROSCon 2026 in Toronto on 22 September.

Read the full itemCollapse

Intrinsic, Alphabet's industrial robotics software company, released Intrinsic Core as open source under an Apache 2.0 licence at ROSCon 2026 in Toronto on 22 September. The package contains a hardware-agnostic real-time control framework, six-degree-of-freedom pose estimation built on Nvidia's FoundationPose, motion and grasp planning, simulation services via Gazebo, camera calibration and ROS-compatible drivers. The code is on GitHub from launch day.

What is being released is, according to Intrinsic, the same building blocks the company uses in live manufacturing deployments, not a stripped-down developer version. An open reference solution is published alongside it, the Open Machine Tending Solution for robotic tending of CNC machines, customisable to hardware from Japan's Fanuc and Denmark's Universal Robots. Intrinsic describes the aim as democratising access to intelligent robotics and points out that only a small share of the tens of thousands of shops fabricating parts in the US and Europe have any automation at all. The company itself notes that Intrinsic Core still requires basic robotics proficiency. That Alphabet is giving the infrastructure away also says something about where the value is assumed to sit: in the models and cloud services above, not in the control layer.

For the Nordics: this hits Nordic engineering industry at the sore point — the integration work, not the capital. According to Intrinsic the reference solution can be adapted to hardware from Denmark's Universal Robots, and both ABB Robotics and Universal Robots have software layers of their own in the niche that now becomes free. For Swedish and Norwegian subcontractors with ten to fifty employees, the barrier has never been the robots but the work of getting them to do something useful, and a pre-configured layer that can be run locally on your own hardware lowers exactly that barrier. Sintef, NTNU and KTH at the same time get an industrially proven platform to do research on at no licence cost.

DR and NRK pulled spy documentary for fear AI could unmask anonymous sources

Danish DR and Norwegian NRK have removed the documentary series ”Skyggekrigen” from their streaming services for fear that AI tools could identify anonymous sources in the programmes, NRK said on Monday.

Read the full itemCollapse

Danish DR and Norwegian NRK have removed the documentary series ”Skyggekrigen” from their streaming services for fear that AI tools could identify anonymous sources in the programmes, NRK said on Monday. The series first aired in 2023, was produced by four Nordic public broadcasters and deals with Russian intelligence activity in northern Europe — including the claim that at least 50 Russian civilian vessels may have been used to gather information covertly, and that ships mapped sensitive infrastructure ahead of possible sabotage.

NRK took the series down when DR informed it that they had done the same, according to Frode Frøyland, editor-in-chief of NRK's documentary programme Brennpunkt. He also states that NRK anonymised the material in a way he describes as completely secure at first publication, and that the broadcaster is now in the process of putting the series back online. Morten Goodwin, professor of artificial intelligence at the University of Agder, tells NRK that AI has become very good at de-anonymising content — images, sound, movement and video — in cases where the anonymisation was done poorly. His conclusion is that face and surroundings must be blurred completely, not partially, and the voice altered fully; if anonymisation is done properly, there is in his view nothing to fear. DR had not responded to a request for comment from the news agency AFP.

For the Nordics: published material is being withdrawn by a Nordic public broadcaster with AI as the stated reason, and that makes source protection a technical question and not only a legal one. Source protection in Norway and the anonymity protection in Sweden's Freedom of the Press Act both rest on an editorial team being able to keep a promise over time — but a promise given in 2023 is now tested against tools that did not exist then. For SVT, NRK, DR and Yle that means the whole archive, not just new productions, needs reassessing. And one question travels on to the Norwegian and Swedish data protection authorities: when a tool can link a de-identified face to a named person, who is then processing the personal data?

Source: NRK, Aftenposten, AFP/The Local

Norway is raising safe and controlled AI as one of its priority areas as the 81st UN General Assembly opens its high-level week in New York today. The Norwegian AI act was planned for adoption in spring 2026 but has been delayed by EEA adaptation, and the government now aims to put a bill before the Storting in spring 2027. Nkom has been designated the national coordinating supervisory authority, while Datatilsynet takes responsibility within its own areas. That means Norwegian organisations have an international position to lean on for another year or so but no national rulebook to follow. The same pattern applies in Sweden: IMY has named AI in the public sector a supervision priority for 2026, but the binding text is written in Brussels and – if the panel is heeded – in New York.

Quantum

IBM combines error detection and error cancellation – sampling shrinks up to 63-fold

IBM researchers have demonstrated a hybrid method that layers probabilistic error cancellation on top of quantum error detection.

Read the full itemCollapse

IBM researchers have demonstrated a hybrid method that layers probabilistic error cancellation on top of quantum error detection. The method models faults according to both where and when they occur in the circuit and builds a representation of the noise that incorporates the circuit’s syndrome information. The work was posted to arXiv in mid-September and drew wider coverage on 21 September.

The problem it addresses is that post-selection creates relationships between faults at different points in the circuit: a single detectable fault normally causes the run to be discarded, while two faults can produce syndromes that cancel each other so the run passes the checks with an error still in it. On the ibm_aachen processor the estimated sampling overhead came out 3.7 times lower than error cancellation without detection at two Trotter steps, 15.9 times lower at four and 63 times lower at six. The 63-fold figure therefore applies to the deepest circuit in the experiment, not to the method in general. According to IBM’s roadmap, as reported by Dataconomy, at least 1,000 programmable qubits are to be connected in 2027 on the path toward the fault-tolerant machine Starling in 2029.

For the Nordics: Sampling overhead is the practical bottleneck for Nordic research groups that buy quantum time by the hour rather than own the hardware. The Wallenberg Centre for Quantum Technology at Chalmers builds its own superconducting quantum computers and can test the method on its own hardware, while groups at KTH, NTNU and the Niels Bohr Institute in Copenhagen more often run via cloud services where every extra sampling round costs money. Finland’s IQM, which sells superconducting systems to European research centres, meanwhile gains a selling point about software rather than qubit count – a more level field for the Nordic players who will never match IBM on scale.

Source: arXiv 2609.13108, The Quantum Insider, Quantum Computing Report, Dataconomy

Advertised pay in the US occupations most exposed to generative AI has risen by 46 percent since 2021, against 41 percent for moderately exposed roles and 25 percent for the least exposed. For the Nordics: The pattern – a premium at the top, pressure at the entry point – matches what the Nordic social partners have measured but not priced. Research

Alibaba’s research institute releases a CT model for research – it beat 23 of 26 radiologists

Alibaba’s DAMO Academy has released RADAR, a model that reads abdominal CT scans and screens for 146 findings across 18 organs.

Read the full itemCollapse

Alibaba’s DAMO Academy has released RADAR, a model that reads abdominal CT scans and screens for 146 findings across 18 organs. The code sits on GitHub under Apache 2.0, while the model weights themselves are released under CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 – a licence that allows research but not commercial deployment without a separate agreement. The release came on 18 September, alongside the publication of the group’s paper in Science.

The model was trained on more than 400,000 CT examinations and 15 million image-report pairs without manual annotation. Tested on nearly 40,000 real-world examinations it reached an average AUC of 0.913 across the 146 clinical findings. In a sub-study with 26 radiologists from several hospitals the model on average performed better than 23 of the participants, and when the radiologists used RADAR as an assistant missed diagnoses fell by 10 percent while time per diagnosis dropped by more than 30 percent.

For the Nordics: Open weights move the question from procurement to accountability – but only one step. Radiologist shortages and long turnaround times are known problems in both Swedish and Norwegian health care, and a model running on the hospital’s own hardware means patient images never leave the hospital network, exactly the kind of data minimisation IMY and Datatilsynet point to when AI is used in care. The licence, however, closes the door to clinical deployment without an agreement with Alibaba, the training data comes from Chinese centres, and CE marking as a medical device is an entirely different process from downloading weights. After the Digital Omnibus, the AI Act’s high-risk obligations are also deferred – to 2 December 2027 for standalone Annex III systems and to 2 August 2028 for high-risk AI embedded in products under sectoral legislation. For Swedish regions and Norwegian health trusts, RADAR is therefore a research tool today, not a procurable product.

Anthropic announced on Friday that the consulting giant Accenture will become the company's first embedded evaluator, a first step toward the commitment to external evaluators inside AI companies that CEO Dario Amodei made in his essay “We Must Pace the Frontier.” The work will be led by Faculty, the British AI company Accenture acquired in January, and covers red-teaming of models, alignment assessments and testing of safeguards. Anthropic and Accenture each expect to invest at least a billion dollars in building up the capacity over five years, and Accenture's shares rose eight percent in after-hours trading, according to TechCrunch.

Unlike today's external evaluators, the embedded evaluators are to work inside the company with access comparable to an employee's: they are to be able to follow models during training, see the decisions that govern how they are built and deployed, speak directly with staff and report incidents. Anthropic writes that there is as yet no standard for what information the evaluators should have access to or how they should report, and that funding should in the long run come from pooled or public sources; for now Anthropic pays Accenture directly, while Anthropic says it is in dialogue with METR and other nonprofit evaluators about piloting elements of the arrangement with their own funding. The choice surprised the industry, which had expected safety researchers such as METR, Redwood Research or Apollo Research; TechCrunch notes that Accenture, as a large public company, is more independent of Anthropic than the tight-knit AI safety community, but also that critics see the whole scheme as a way for the industry to police itself.

For the Nordics, this is the first concrete answer to the comparison Prime Minister Jonas Gahr Støre drew in NTB this week: if three chief executives in the defense industry had said they were losing control of what they produce, politics would have stepped in, and he is prepared to raise AI safety at the UN General Assembly next week. Bård Standal at the law firm Schjødt points out in Digi.no that a global rulebook could take many years because the US and China will always suspect each other of cheating, and in the meantime the companies' own evaluators are what exists. For the Swedish Authority for Privacy Protection, Datatilsynet and the EU's AI Office, which is to assess models with systemic risk, the question becomes whether a paid consultancy with employee-level access counts as independent – and Accenture also has an extensive Nordic consulting business with clients in both the public sector and industry, which makes the independence question concrete here too.
