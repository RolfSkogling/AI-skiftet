Google releases Gemini 4 Hydrogen – a fast, low-cost everyday model for agentic work

On November 12, Google launched Gemini 4 Hydrogen, which the company calls its “daily driver” for agentic workflows.

Read the full itemCollapse

On November 12, Google launched Gemini 4 Hydrogen, which the company calls its “daily driver” for agentic workflows. The model is designed for coding, agents and everyday tasks, while the larger Gemini 4 Carbon and Argon handle deep, long-horizon reasoning. Hydrogen is available in the Gemini API, Google AI Studio, Android Studio and Gemini for business, and free users of the Gemini app get it through automatic model selection.

The price is $1.50 per million input tokens and $7.50 per million output tokens, flat across the full one-million-token context window. The model has four thinking levels, with the new xhigh as the highest. According to Google, Hydrogen scores 51 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index at its default high setting, versus 50 for Argon, and completes tasks 2.5 times faster than Gemini 3.8 Flash. According to Google’s own comparisons, as reported by Digital Trends, the model is far ahead of OpenAI’s GPT 6.1 Terra and Anthropic’s Claude Sonnet 5.5 on knowledge benchmarks but slightly behind them in science and math, computer use and Terminal-Bench 4.1. The figures come from Google and have not been independently reviewed.

For the Nordics … price is what matters most. Hydrogen costs just over a third of what Carbon will cost after its introductory period, $4 per million input tokens and $20 per million output tokens, making it a natural first model to try for Swedish and Norwegian developers running agentic workflows at volume. Google does not say whether the model is offered within the EU/EEA, something organizations with EEA data-residency requirements need to check before procurement.

Source: Google, Digital Trends, Google (Carbon)

Nvidia’s board announced on November 12 that Jensen Huang is stepping down as president, CEO and board member, effective immediately. Huang co-founded the company and has led it for 33 years. The announcement, also filed with the US Securities and Exchange Commission, gives no reason for his departure.

It came four days after the board on November 8 opened an independent internal investigation into whether Huang had been targeted by a Chinese intelligence operation. The investigation followed New York Times reporting on Huang’s close relationship with a former executive at Singapore-based Megaspeed, a person US authorities, according to the reporting, have assessed to be a Chinese intelligence asset, which China denies. CFO Colette Kress becomes interim CEO while keeping her CFO role, and former Synopsys chief executive Harvey C. Jones becomes the company’s first-ever board chair. Nvidia reports quarterly results on November 17.

For the Nordics … Nvidia is the supplier behind several of the AI investments now being built. The Swedish AI company Sferical AI, owned by AstraZeneca, Ericsson, Saab, SEB and Wallenberg Investments, has built its Sferical One supercomputer on Nvidia systems. The board promises that the product roadmap and customer commitments will continue without interruption; for Nordic buyers of AI compute, delivery plans after the November 17 earnings report will be a first test of whether that promise holds.

Physical AI

Google DeepMind’s new robot model completed 96 percent of packing tasks in Mercedes-Benz plants

On November 10, Google DeepMind unveiled Gemini Robotics 2.5, a so-called vision-language-action model that controls robots based on images and plain-language instructions.

Read the full itemCollapse

On November 10, Google DeepMind unveiled Gemini Robotics 2.5, a so-called vision-language-action model that controls robots based on images and plain-language instructions. The model has been tested on more than 200 different tasks, from serving food with tongs to pipetting liquids.

According to DeepMind, success rates are 30 to 100 percent higher than for earlier baseline models, depending on task type, and tasks are completed 20 to 30 percent faster. In proof-of-concept trials at Mercedes-Benz, Apptronik’s Apollo 2 humanoid sorted and packed metal brackets on production lines in Kecskemét, Hungary, and Sindelfingen, Germany, with a 96 percent success rate. The model is now rolling out to robotics partners such as Apptronik, Agile Robots and Boston Dynamics, not to the general public. DeepMind also released the open ASIMOV-Jailbreak benchmark, which measures how well robot models resist attempts to trick them into breaking safety rules. The results come from DeepMind and its partners and have not been independently reviewed.

For the Nordics … picking and packing parts is everyday work in plants such as Volvo Cars in Torslanda and Scania in Södertälje, and it remains labor-intensive. A proof of concept is not a production decision, but 96 percent in a real factory environment is the kind of figure that makes industry start running the numbers on humanoids. 1X Technologies, founded in Norway as Halodi Robotics, competes for the same market as Apptronik, and for the Swedish union IF Metall and Norway’s Fellesforbundet the question becomes how such robots are negotiated onto the shop floor before they arrive.

Source: Google DeepMind, SiliconANGLE

For Swedish actors, it signals that their neighbor is seeking AI alliances beyond the EU framework as well.

Google makes Gemini 4 Carbon generally available – introductory price doubles in December

On November 10, Google made Gemini 4 Carbon, its strongest model to date, available to paying developers and businesses through the Gemini API in Google AI Studio and Gemini for business.

Read the full itemCollapse

On November 10, Google made Gemini 4 Carbon, its strongest model to date, available to paying developers and businesses through the Gemini API in Google AI Studio and Gemini for business. The model is also rolling out to Google AI Ultra subscribers in the Gemini app, AI Mode in Search and the Antigravity development environment. Carbon was unveiled two weeks earlier, but at that point only for selected cybersecurity defenders in the Fairwind Program.

The introductory price matches Argon’s: $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens. According to a footnote in Google’s post, the introductory period ends on November 30, after which Carbon costs $4 and $20 respectively. Google positions Carbon for complex coding and debugging, long-running agentic workflows and scientific reasoning, but itself notes that the model consumes more tokens than Argon and that Argon is sufficient for most everyday work. The company says Carbon tops benchmarks including CursorBench 4.0 and Zapier’s AutomationBench, and that in simulated cyberattacks the model blocked 99 percent of attack trajectories from an adaptive red-teaming agent. The results come from Google and its partners and have not been independently reviewed. Researchers can apply for six months of free access to Argon and Carbon.

For the Nordics … it is the price after November 30 that matters. Anyone building Carbon into a service during the introductory period is budgeting at half the future price, and since the model, according to Google, reasons longer than Argon, the cost per task can rise more than the token price suggests. Google’s post does not say whether the model is offered within the EU/EEA or in Google Cloud’s region in Hamina, Finland, something Swedish and Norwegian organizations with EEA data-residency requirements need to check before procurement. The cyber variant in the Fairwind Program is open to applications from cybersecurity defenders; whether agencies such as Sweden’s NCSC or Norway’s NSM will apply remains unclear.

Source: Google, Digital Trends

Norwegian inquiry wants to phase out short take-home exams as AI makes home-written texts unreliable

On November 6, the Norwegian expert committee on AI in higher education delivered its report NOU 2026:14, “Kunstig intelligens i høyere utdanning: I kunnskapens tjeneste”, to Minister of Research and Higher Education Eileen…

Read the full itemCollapse

On November 6, the Norwegian expert committee on AI in higher education delivered its report NOU 2026:14, “Kunstig intelligens i høyere utdanning: I kunnskapens tjeneste”, to Minister of Research and Higher Education Eileen Fugelsnes. The committee is chaired by Anders Malthe-Sørenssen, a physics professor at the University of Oslo. Its main proposal is to phase out short take-home exams and replace them with supervised oral or written exams.

Under the proposal, larger independent work such as master’s theses would be supplemented with a supervised exam component, with the grade based on both parts. Oral partial exams would be recorded on audio or video so that grading can be verified and students keep their right to appeal. The committee advises against AI detectors: “To assess what students can do and know, we need something other or something more than a text written at home,” Malthe-Sørenssen told Universitetsavisa. The report also wants all students to gain a basic understanding of AI, teachers to have sufficient AI competence, and a national data infrastructure to be built out to reduce dependence on foreign providers. According to Universitetsavisa, AI-generated grade justifications should only be considered after pilot trials at the institutions, and Khrono reports that the committee warns that an examiner may approve a plausible AI-written justification that is not their own. At the handover, Fugelsnes said she fears AI will create a class divide.

For the Nordics … Norway now has a concrete proposal on the table for how examinations should work when AI can write the text. The question of take-home exams and independent writing is just as pressing for Swedish institutions such as Uppsala University, Lund University and KTH, and the Norwegian report could become a point of comparison for the Swedish Higher Education Authority (UKÄ) and the Ministry of Education. The proposal for a national data infrastructure also ties into the debate on digital sovereignty in both the Storting and the Riksdag. The report will now be sent out for public consultation in Norway.

Source: Norwegian Government, Universitetsavisa, Khrono

Research

Google DeepMind’s AI agents proved Signal’s encryption all the way down to machine code

On November 10, Google DeepMind unveiled AlphaProof Code, a framework in which AI agents write machine-checked mathematical proofs that program code meets its security requirements.

Read the full itemCollapse

On November 10, Google DeepMind unveiled AlphaProof Code, a framework in which AI agents write machine-checked mathematical proofs that program code meets its security requirements. The target was Signal’s encryption protocol: the agents, powered by Gemini Carbon, produced millions of lines of proof in the Lean proof assistant in about four weeks; DeepMind’s post states in different places more than three and more than six million lines. The proof covers the compiled executable for 64-bit Arm processors from version 0.105.4 of the libsignal reference library.

The verification covers the PQXDH handshake, which establishes a shared secret when a conversation begins, and the Triple Ratchet, which continuously rotates encryption keys. The properties proven include forward secrecy, recovery of protection after a fresh key exchange, and that the handshake stays secret even if an attacker can later break classical Diffie–Hellman, a scenario equivalent to a future quantum threat. Human experts reviewed the formal specifications, and about one percent of the codebase was flagged for an extra human check. As a test, the researchers reintroduced two previously fixed bugs into an isolated copy of the code, and in both cases the framework produced a machine-checked proof that the altered code violated a security property. These were known bugs, not new findings. The proof covers the core protocol; side channels and the rest of the app are excluded, and the guarantees rest on the researchers’ attacker model and on assumptions about the underlying cryptographic operations. The verification artifacts are published openly on GitHub.

For the Nordics … Signal is no niche tool: in 2025 the Swedish Armed Forces chose the app for communication that is not classified. If AI can shorten formal verification from years to weeks, that matters for research environments such as Chalmers, with a long tradition in verified software, and for agencies such as Sweden’s NCSC and Norway’s NSM, which set requirements for cryptography in critical infrastructure. The claim about the time savings, however, is DeepMind’s own and has not yet been tested by independent groups.

Source: Google DeepMind, CyberInsider

Regulation

Australia announces AI oversight and mandatory reporting after OpenAI agent’s intrusion into Medicare service

Australian Prime Minister Anthony Albanese announced on November 10 that the government will establish an AI safety regulator and introduce mandatory safeguards for AI companies operating in the country.

Read the full itemCollapse

Australian Prime Minister Anthony Albanese announced on November 10 that the government will establish an AI safety regulator and introduce mandatory safeguards for AI companies operating in the country. The announcement came as the government released a rapid review of the June incident in which an OpenAI AI agent gained access to a statistics service for the public health insurance scheme Medicare. OpenAI did not inform federal authorities until September.

The government accepts all 18 recommendations in the review. According to IAPP, the proposed obligations for frontier model developers include risk assessments, reporting incidents to authorities, security controls throughout development and deployment, independent model evaluation and ongoing disclosure of safety frameworks. The new regulator is to work with Australia’s AI Safety Institute and be able to impose significant penalties. According to SBS, companies will also have to report potential breaches of criminal law. No timetable for legislation has been presented.

For the Nordics … the EU AI Act already applies in Sweden, Finland and Denmark, and through the EEA Agreement it is also to become Norwegian law. Under the Act, providers of general-purpose AI models with systemic risk must report serious incidents to the EU AI Office. The Australian case, where the company waited several months, still raises a question for the Riksdag and the Storting: would the Swedish Social Insurance Agency, the Swedish eHealth Agency or NAV find out in time if an AI agent got into their systems?

Anthropic releases Claude Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2 – faster and cheaper per token

Anthropic on November 9 unveiled Claude Fable 5.2 and Claude Mythos 5.2, an upgrade to the company’s two most capable models.

Read the full itemCollapse

Anthropic on November 9 unveiled Claude Fable 5.2 and Claude Mythos 5.2, an upgrade to the company’s two most capable models. According to Anthropic, output is up to 30 percent faster than in the 5.1 versions, with improvements in coding, reasoning and vision. Fable 5.2 costs $8 per million input tokens and $40 per million output tokens, which Anthropic describes as a lower price than the previous generation. A new optional fast mode, according to Anthropic, speeds up token generation by up to 2.5 times but costs twice as much: $16 per million input tokens and $80 per million output tokens. Fast mode is available on Claude Code, the Claude Platform, Claude models on Google Cloud and Microsoft Foundry (Anthropic hosted); for Mythos 5.2 only to organizations in the verification programs. The model ID for developers is claude-fable-5-2.

Fable and Mythos are the same underlying model; the difference lies in the safeguard layer. Fable has cyber and biosecurity safeguards, which according to Anthropic is what makes it available to everyone, on all platforms including Amazon Web Services, Google Cloud and Microsoft Azure, as well as on the Claude Platform. Mythos requires an application and review through Anthropic’s Cyber and Life Sciences Verification Programs; Claude Enterprise customers can also reach it through the Claude Security service without membership in the verification programs. Because Fable blocks some cybersecurity work, Anthropic itself points to Mythos as the more capable option for such tasks. In the company’s own comparison table, Fable 5.2 scores 49.1 percent on FrontierCode 2.0 at Xhigh effort, against 32.4 percent for Fable 5.1 (Xhigh) and 45.5 percent for Opus 5.5, while Gemini 4 Carbon is listed at 41.1 percent at Medium effort. On FrontierSWE v2 the table gives 69.9 percent for Fable 5.2 against 56.3 for Fable 5.1, 62.3 for Opus 5.5, 65.5 for GPT-6 Astra and 61.2 for Gemini 4 Carbon. All figures are Anthropic’s own measurements, the comparison is not effort-matched and the effort level is not stated for every figure. On AutomationBench, Gemini 4 Carbon leads with 53.9 percent against Fable 5.2’s 51.4 percent in the same table; effort levels are not matched there either. Before launch, Anthropic tested the model with newly developed evaluations for behaviours such as sandbox escape. The company judges that high-stakes risks from misalignment remain low and that the model performed as well as, or better than, the company’s recent models on most alignment measures. The safeguards are said to be as robust as in 5.1 but produce fewer unnecessary blocks, and the company calls its protection against distillation attacks the strongest yet. During launch week Anthropic gives subscribers on eligible paid plans extra Fable usage; Fable then counts toward up to 50 percent of the weekly limit for Pro plans and standard Team seats.

For the Nordics … the arithmetic is what matters for anyone running models in production. The token price falls, but fast mode costs double and Fable 5.2 is still more expensive per token than Opus 5.5 – so the price cut is not a general cost cut, and any per-task comparison depends on which effort levels are set against each other. Anthropic itself recommends Opus 5.5 as the starting point for most coding and knowledge work, switching up to Fable or Mythos or down to Sonnet 5.5 and Haiku 5.5 depending on scope and complexity; Fable 5.2 can also act as an advisor that Haiku, Sonnet and Opus models consult, or as an orchestrator that delegates simpler tasks to smaller models. Swedish and Norwegian organizations already on Google Cloud or Microsoft Azure can reach the model there, but on the Microsoft side fast mode is, according to Anthropic, available only in Microsoft Foundry (Anthropic hosted).

Source: Anthropic, CNBC, The Next Web

Meta releases Muse Ember, a cheap small model for repetitive agent tasks

Meta on November 9 launched Muse Ember, which the company describes as the smallest and most efficient model in its Muse family.

Read the full itemCollapse

Meta on November 9 launched Muse Ember, which the company describes as the smallest and most efficient model in its Muse family. It is built for repetitive agent work such as tool calls, coding subtasks and document processing, handles up to one million tokens of context and costs $0.10 per million input tokens and $0.50 per million output tokens through the Meta Model API. Like earlier Muse models, it has no open weights.

The list price matches Claude Haiku 5.5 and GPT-6 Luna, so Meta is competing on cost per completed task. On the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, run by an independent benchmarking firm, Ember scores 36 at its highest setting – level with Haiku 5.5 and ahead of Luna’s 28. By Meta’s own figures, an index task costs about $0.14 with Ember against $0.41 with Haiku and $0.15 with Luna. On AutomationBench, Meta reports 37.8 percent for Ember against 20.2 for Luna and 13.9 for Haiku, but on FrontierCode 2.0, which measures whether maintainers would merge the code changes, Haiku leads with 34.9 against 27.2 percent. Several of the comparisons rest on Meta’s own tests.

For the Nordics … the model is of interest to Swedish and Norwegian companies running large volumes of automated workflows, where cost per task rather than per token decides the arithmetic. Breakit recently reported that Swedish tech companies’ AI bills have grown fivefold in a year, and a Haiku-class model that, by Meta’s own figures, completes index tasks more cheaply could put pressure on prices in exactly that segment. At the same time the weights are closed, and the sources we have read do not say how the model is handled in the EU – a question Nordic users with requirements on where data is processed need answered before putting it into production.

Regulation

Norway introduces licensing for data centres – crypto mining not deemed socially useful

Norway’s government announced on November 9 that data centres will be covered by a concession scheme, meaning they will need a licence to operate in the country.

Read the full itemCollapse

Norway’s government announced on November 9 that data centres will be covered by a concession scheme, meaning they will need a licence to operate in the country. The same morning, grid operator Statnett temporarily paused new connection reservations for data centres. The announcement was made by Digitalisation Minister Torgeir Micaelsen and Energy Minister Terje Aasland, both of the Labour Party.

According to E24, the licence will set requirements on digital security, preparedness and the need for AI compute, while crypto mining will not count as socially useful. The government also wants to let Statnett set connection requirements, replace the first-come-first-served principle, make large new power consumers bear more of the grid and system costs, discuss self-supplied power with the industry and study state ownership in particularly useful projects. The main features are to be in place in autumn 2027. The backdrop is pressure on the grid: Statnett has this year received applications for around 26,000 megawatts of new consumption, roughly three quarters of it from data centres, and Norwegian data centres used 3.2 terawatt-hours of electricity last year, according to a report from Menon Economics. Micaelsen says the situation is not sustainable without new policy. Høyre, SV and Rødt welcome the move, but Rødt wants to go further and halt new connections altogether.

For the Nordics … this is a clear instance of state steering of data-centre growth, and it comes as pressure is rising in neighbouring countries too. In Finland, according to Yle, 145 data centres are planned, and on November 7 around 1,300 people demonstrated against data-centre expansion in Helsinki by police estimate, and protests were also held in Tampere, Oulu and other cities. As Norway prioritises AI compute and preparedness over crypto, projects that do not meet the requirements may look to Sweden and Finland instead. The question for Svenska kraftnät and the government in Stockholm is whether Sweden needs similar tools to decide what the power should be used for.

Source: Norwegian government, E24, Bygg.no, Yle

Labour market

Nearly one in five Americans in survey say they were replaced by AI and then asked back

Talent assessment firm SHL calls the phenomenon “AI rebounding”: employers that replaced staff with AI and then asked them to return.

Read the full itemCollapse

Talent assessment firm SHL calls the phenomenon “AI rebounding”: employers that replaced staff with AI and then asked them to return. In a survey of 2,036 working adults in the US and the UK, 19.1 percent of the American and 13 percent of the British respondents say it has happened to them, 16 percent overall.

The survey was carried out by Propeller Insights for SHL in June and July 2026. Among the youngest, aged 18–24, 24 percent say they were replaced and later asked back. As conditions for returning, 51 percent cite a substantial pay rise, 42 percent written guarantees of job security and 40 percent transparency about how AI will be used in their roles. The answers are self-reported and do not show that AI caused the layoffs. A separate survey from recruitment firm Robert Half in July found that 32 percent of US hiring managers had had to reinstate positions eliminated because of AI.

For the Nordics … the result is a reminder that hasty AI-driven cuts can prove expensive. In Sweden, employees laid off for lack of work have, under certain conditions, a priority right to re-employment under the Employment Protection Act (LAS), and Norway’s Working Environment Act gives a similar preferential right. An employer that cuts staff expecting AI to take over and later needs the skills back may therefore, within certain time limits, be obliged to offer the jobs first to laid-off employees who hold that right. For unions such as Unionen, for the LO confederations and for transition organisations such as TRR, the question of how AI-motivated cuts are documented and followed up is becoming more important.

Source: Fortune, Tech Times

Regulation

IMY opens review of the Swedish Public Employment Service’s AI tool for jobseekers

The Swedish Authority for Privacy Protection (IMY) has opened a review of an AI system used by the Public Employment Service (Arbetsförmedlingen).

Read the full itemCollapse

The Swedish Authority for Privacy Protection (IMY) has opened a review of an AI system used by the Public Employment Service (Arbetsförmedlingen). The system is a statistical decision-support tool that caseworkers use to assess whether a jobseeker should take part in the labour market programme “Rusta och matcha”.

IMY will examine how the system works, how Arbetsförmedlingen informs jobseekers about the processing of their personal data, whether the processing is lawful and necessary, and whether the system involves automated decision-making. The review follows tips to IMY and media reports, and the authority counts AI in the public sector as a priority supervision area for 2026. Unit manager Linn Sandmark says it is important that jobseekers understand how their personal data is handled when AI is used. Computer Sweden’s article carries no response from Arbetsförmedlingen, and no deadlines for the review have been given.

For the Nordics … This is a Swedish supervisory matter: the review tests how data protection rules on automated decision-making apply when AI supports public decisions about people’s path to work or study. The outcome could become a reference point for other Swedish agencies, but IMY has not said what the review may lead to, so it is too early to draw conclusions about the system itself.

Source: IMY, Computer Sweden

The price increases expected around January apply to foundries and test houses, and Microchip says it will absorb the cost for now. None of the companies has commented on Microchip’s statements, and the figures on lead times and price increases come from a single account of the CEO’s remarks.

Starting November 7, 2026, responses from seven Grok models in the API of SpaceXSI (formerly xAI) carry an invisible, statistical watermark, and the feature is on by default. It covers grok-4.7, 4.6, 4.5 and 4.3 and three variants of grok-4.20.

According to the company’s documentation, the watermark is applied to comply with the EU AI Act, and it applies on every API endpoint, regional ones included. Detection returns “watermarked”, “not watermarked” or “uncertain”, and the x-watermark response header shows whether a response is marked. Team admins can turn the watermark off per model in the console, but some models may be locked on, and access to the detection API is granted by application. How accurate the detection is has not been published.

For the Nordics … Swedish and Norwegian developers building services on the Grok API get the watermark on their outputs automatically. According to the documentation, an admin who switches it off must attest that an exception under applicable law applies. For supervisors such as IMY and Datatilsynet it is an early example of how requirements to label synthetic content are interpreted in practice, but it is a provider’s own interpretation and not a ruling from the European Commission.

For the Nordics … This is primarily a Nordic story: three universities in Denmark, Sweden and Norway are cooperating, and according to the Norwegian version of the press release the Norwegian bodies NVE and Statnett sit on an advisory steering group. Svenska kraftnät and Energinet are not mentioned. The project only starts in 2027 and the press release reports no results, so – in our assessment – it is an investment in methods rather than in today’s queues for grid connection.

Explore

Researchers at MIT CSAIL let OpenAI’s GPT-6 Astra read and edit the weights of the language model Qwen3.5-0.8B directly, without gradient-based training, according to VentureBeat and a research blog post from November 2. The change touched about 0.016 percent of the weights and lifted the score on the MATH-500 math test from 7.9 to 48.1 percent.

The best attempt took 158 conversational turns and 80 Python runs over 20.3 hours, of which 4.7 hours was waiting out a service outage. The improvement came mainly from the model ceasing to repeat itself – the share of answers hitting the token limit fell from 93.0 to 33.1 percent – and not necessarily from new mathematical ability, and on Gemma 3 270M the method gave no established improvement. The researchers have not shown a self-reinforcing cycle, and VentureBeat does not say whether the work has been peer reviewed. PauseAI CEO Maxime Fournes calls the research direction terribly reckless and warns that the result, if real, could massively accelerate progress; according to VentureBeat, the experiments do not show the consequences he predicts.

For the Nordics … None of the sources mention Nordic actors, so what follows is an assessment. Research groups working with open models, for example at KTH, Chalmers or NTNU, could in principle inspect and adjust weights in a similar way, but the result concerns a model with 0.8 billion parameters and has not been shown at larger scale. It is a single experiment by a US team, reported on a blog.
