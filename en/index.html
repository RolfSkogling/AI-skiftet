Physical AI

SoftBank in talks for a majority stake in Norwegian robot maker 1X

SoftBank is in talks to buy a majority stake in the Norwegian humanoid company 1X Technologies at a valuation of roughly $6 billion, according to reporting by The Information.

1X was founded in 2014 in Moss by roboticist Bernt Øivind Børnich under the name Halodi Robotics and rebranded in 2023, the same year the OpenAI Startup Fund invested alongside Tiger Global and a group of Norwegian investors. The company builds the Neo humanoid for the home and opened its factory in Hayward, California, earlier this year. The $6 billion valuation sits below the $10 billion the company was reported to have sought last autumn. Talks are ongoing and terms may change — neither party has confirmed a deal.

For the Nordics this is close to home. In October 2025 SoftBank agreed to buy ABB's robotics division for $5.375 billion, a transaction that has not yet closed and is expected to complete during 2026. If the 1X deal also goes through, the same Japanese owner would hold both one of the Nordics' heaviest industrial robotics legacies and the region's most talked-about humanoid company. Innovation Norway and the Research Council of Norway have funded the underlying robotics expertise at SINTEF and NTNU for decades — the question for Norwegian owners and authorities becomes returns now versus control of the technology later.

Source: The Information, Reuters, ABB
Regulation

Court strikes down the Pentagon's blacklisting of Anthropic

A federal judge in California ruled this week that the Defense Department's decision to label Anthropic a supply chain risk was unlawful, and ordered the designation removed.

The dispute began when Anthropic refused to lift its own restrictions on autonomous weapons and domestic mass surveillance in a contract with the Pentagon. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth responded on 27 February by designating the company a supply chain risk, limiting the ability of agencies and defense contractors to do business with Anthropic. Judge Rita Lin wrote that the designation was unlawful retaliation in violation of the First Amendment, and that the company had also been denied the process the Fifth Amendment requires. An empty invocation of national security is no blank check to punish government critics, she wrote. The government may appeal.

The Nordics face the same question, still unanswered: who sets the limits on military AI, the buyer or the lab? FMV in Sweden and the Norwegian Defence Materiel Agency increasingly procure AI from the same handful of American suppliers. Saab and Kongsberg build autonomous systems in which a supplier's terms of use can become an operational constraint in the middle of a mission. The difference from the US is the size of the negotiating table: a Nordic agency does not carry the same weight against an American supplier, and the terms arrive effectively pre-written.

Source: CNBC, NPR, TechCrunch, CNN
Quantum

Australian quantum company bets on silicon chips from ordinary semiconductor fabs

Diraq published a white paper this week setting out a roadmap toward utility-scale quantum computers built on spin qubits in silicon, manufactured in the same CMOS processes as today's ordinary processors.

The roadmap targets 150,000 physical qubits and up to 1,000 logical qubits by 2029, then more than two million physical and over 10,000 logical qubits by 2031. The company sets four conditions for commercial viability: scalability to millions of qubits, a cost below one dollar per qubit, deployment inside existing data centres, and working error correction. Diraq has previously demonstrated qubit arrays fabricated at the Belgian research institute imec. This is a roadmap and a commitment — none of the targets have been met.

For the Nordics the point is the technology bet. Chalmers and WACQT build superconducting qubits and are currently at 25 qubits with a target of 100 by 2029, IQM in Finland also builds superconducting systems, and the Niels Bohr Institute in Copenhagen works on semiconductor-based qubits. If silicon turns out to win the scaling race, the question for the Wallenberg Foundations, Vinnova and the Research Council of Norway is not only more money, but whether Nordic semiconductor and process expertise — the kind found at Chalmers, KTH and NTNU — is connected to the quantum side in time.

Source: Diraq, HPCwire, Chalmers WACQT
Debate

Actors demand legal protection for their voices in the UK

Around eighty actors and other public figures have signed an open letter to the British government demanding that the voice be recognised as a protected part of a person's identity.

Signatories include Nicola Coughlan, Matt Lucas, Hugh Bonneville, Luke Evans and Pearl Mackie. The Save Our Voices Now campaign, driven forward by audiobook narrator Alice Sockett, points out that current systems can recreate a voice from only a few seconds of audio and then put invented statements in a person's mouth. The campaign cites survey data in which 28 percent of UK adults say they have been targeted by voice-cloning scams. The demand is aimed at two things at once: fraudsters posing as relatives or executives, and production companies that can recreate an actor instead of hiring one.

In the Nordics the question has already been settled in one place. After a broad agreement in the Danish parliament, Denmark has legislated a protection giving individuals rights to their own face, body and voice, with compensation available for unauthorised deepfakes and exemptions for satire and parody. The amendment to Danish copyright law was set to enter into force on 31 March 2026, and the Ministry of Culture has presented the framework as a model for a broader European track. For the Swedish and Norwegian parliaments the choice is to follow the Danish line now or wait for the EU — and for Swedish and Norwegian actors' unions, to decide whether collective agreements are enough when a voice can be copied from an old clip.

Source: Variety, Kulturministeriet, DR
Infrastructure

More than a hundred tech companies want a joint defence against AI cyberattacks

More than a hundred companies and organisations have signed an open letter calling for coordinated action against cyber threats in which AI makes the attacker faster and cheaper.

The letter was published on 27 August with OpenAI as its originator, and signatories include Anthropic, Google, Microsoft, Amazon, Cisco, Oracle, Cloudflare, CrowdStrike and Palo Alto Networks, plus companies outside the tech sector such as Mastercard, Visa, General Motors and Shopify. CNBC counted 116 companies and entities. They warn that hospitals, water treatment plants and other critical infrastructure become exposed as models get better at finding vulnerabilities and writing attack code, and they call for a “defensive surge” with training, funding and access to the labs' most capable models for defenders during major incidents. Several signatories are simultaneously building the agents that make the threat larger.

For the Nordics this lands in the middle of work already under way. Sweden and Norway are implementing the EU's NIS2 requirements through the Cybersecurity Act and the Digital Security Act respectively, with MSB and NSM in central roles. It is the regions in Sweden and the regional health enterprises in Norway that run precisely the hospital operations the letter flags as vulnerable, and municipal water utilities in both countries are small organisations with thin IT security staffing. If the standards for AI-driven cyber defence are written in an American industry forum, MSB, NSM and Digdir become recipients rather than co-authors.

Source: TechCrunch, CNBC, Axios

Physical AI

Anthropic opens a standard for AI agents to operate physical machines

On August 27 Anthropic released a research preview of the Model Hardware Standard, MHS — a shared specification for how AI agents should be able to operate physical devices safely.

The preview goes first to a limited group of scientific research labs and advanced manufacturers, which will test how Claude behaves against real equipment and what safeguards are needed. The point is not a product but a shared interface: a way for models and machines to speak the same language regardless of who built which part. The same day the company also presented an expanded effort aimed at scientists. Bloomberg reported on the launch.

For the Nordics this layer is already occupied. 1X Technologies in Moss builds the Neo humanoid, ABB Robotics in Västerås and Universal Robots and MiR in Odense supply industrial robots, Kongsberg develops autonomous systems and robotics research sits at Sintef and NTNU. If the standard for how a model may move a machine is set in California, Nordic engineering and process industry becomes an adopter rather than a co-designer — and anyone who wants to shape it should be in this preview, not the next one.

Source: Anthropic, Bloomberg, Anthropic
Infrastructure

Nvidia reported to buy Hugging Face — where the Nordic open language models live

Nvidia has, according to information given to The Information, agreed to buy Hugging Face for USD 12.9 billion. Neither Nvidia nor Hugging Face has confirmed any agreement.

Hugging Face is the dominant distribution point for open models, datasets and developer tools. The price should be compared with the USD 4.5 billion valuation in the 2023 funding round, in which Nvidia itself took part. The reporting originates with The Information and has been relayed by CNBC, TechCrunch and Fortune among others, and no agreement has been confirmed by the companies. If the deal goes through, the hub for open models ends up owned by the manufacturer that already dominates the hardware those models run on.

For the Nordics this is not an abstract ownership question. The National Library of Norway’s Norwegian language models, NorwAI at NTNU and AI Sweden’s GPT-SW3 are all distributed through Hugging Face, and the same goes for the NB-Whisper speech recognition models. The Nordic line has been that publicly funded language models should be open and independent — yet distribution has rested the whole time on a single American platform. If it becomes a subsidiary of a chipmaker, that is a sharp reason for Vinnova, the Research Council of Norway and the libraries to think through where the models should actually sit.

Source: CNBC, TechCrunch, Fortune, NbAiLab
Regulation

Norway gathers AI guidance and a regulatory sandbox in a single entry point

The Norwegian Digitalisation Agency opens KI Norge in August, a national hub meant to bring together guidance, evidence and a regulatory sandbox for artificial intelligence.

The government has allocated NOK 30 million for the establishment, the sandbox and AI supervision. The sandbox is run jointly by the Digitalisation Agency, the Data Protection Authority and Nkom, but sits organisationally inside KI Norge. Nkom becomes the coordinating market surveillance authority and national contact point for the AI Act and is to help ensure a uniform interpretation and application across the country, while the Data Protection Authority keeps responsibility for personal data questions. The stated target group is small and medium-sized enterprises and newly started companies, which are to receive legal guidance on risks to fundamental rights, health and safety before products reach the market.

For the Nordics this means two different models sitting side by side. Norway gathers guidance and the experimental environment in one organisation and places the coordinating contact point with a single authority, while responsibility in Sweden is split across several authorities, with the Swedish Authority for Privacy Protection, the Post and Telecom Authority and the Financial Supervisory Authority sharing oversight. For a Swedish or Norwegian company selling in both markets, the difference means the same AI system meets one consolidated entry point in Norway and a divided one in Sweden. Norwegian SMEs therefore get a head start on what costs most in practice: knowing who to ask.

Source: Digdir, Nkom, Datatilsynet, KI Norge
Research

AI is said to have designed and verified an AI chip in under two weeks

The startup Architect Labs says an AI system designed and verified the Redwood accelerator in under two weeks. Two human architects wrote the architecture specification.

The circuit currently runs on an FPGA platform, where it performs inference on models with billions of parameters, including Llama and Qwen. The AI system is also said to have designed the firmware and the custom compute kernels. The decisive caveat is that the chip has not yet been manufactured in silicon — manufacturing is where problems with power, timing, yield and reliability tend to appear, and no simulation catches those. The company came out of stealth this summer with USD 24 million in seed funding.

For the Nordics, circuit design is one of the few semiconductor niches where the region is genuinely strong. Nordic Semiconductor in Trondheim makes its living designing low-power chips without owning a fab, Ericsson designs its own silicon for radio networks and Chalmers microwave electronics researches circuit design. If the cost of producing a specialised chip falls from a year to a few weeks, the barrier drops for Nordic companies to build their own — but the same drop applies to every competitor, and the advantage then moves from design capability to access to manufacturing capacity.

Source: SemiWiki, TNW, HPCwire
Labor Market

The US Labor Department takes AI data straight from the tech companies

The US Department of Labor has signed memorandums of understanding with OpenAI, Google, Meta and Amazon among others in order to obtain data on how AI is actually being used at work.

Acting Labor Secretary Keith Sonderling justifies it by saying the department does not have the data itself. Behind it lies a measurement problem: official statistics rest on surveys built for an economy where technology shifts moved more slowly than the AI rollout does now, which makes it hard to separate ordinary business cycles from AI-driven change. The department houses the Bureau of Labor Statistics, whose employment and inflation figures the Federal Reserve uses when setting interest rates. Mixing company data into that basis raises questions about method, representativeness and transparency.

For the Nordics the measurement problem is identical but the decision has not been made. Statistics Sweden’s measurement of AI use in Swedish companies is based on surveys, and the same survey logic underpins the corresponding statistics at the Swedish Public Employment Service, Statistics Norway and Nav. Accepting data instead from the companies whose products drive the change would in both countries be a matter of principle — whether official statistics should rest on material the authority cannot itself examine.

Source: Axios, Statistics Sweden

Hardware

Nvidia’s quarterly revenue doubles in a year — and the company guides for more

On August 26 Nvidia reported revenue of USD 96.2 billion for the quarter that ended on July 26. That is 106 per cent more than a year ago and 18 per cent more than the previous quarter.

Data Center accounted for USD 89.0 billion of the total, up 117 per cent, driven by the Vera Rubin platform reaching full production with racks running at partners including CoreWeave, Google Cloud, Microsoft Azure and Nebius. Gross margin came in at 75.0 per cent and adjusted earnings per share at USD 2.22 against an expected 2.09. For the current quarter the company guides towards USD 108 billion plus or minus 2 per cent, against analyst expectations of roughly 104 billion — and that outlook explicitly assumes no Data Center compute revenue from China at all. The share price still barely moved on the news.

For the Nordics this is not a foreign footnote. As of June 30 Norway’s sovereign wealth fund held just under 1.3 per cent of Nvidia, worth around USD 61.8 billion, making it the fund’s single largest equity holding — every Norwegian and Swedish debate about concentration risk in pension capital now runs through this quarterly report. On home ground the same demand is being built out physically: in Tydal in Trøndelag, Tydal Data Center is raising a facility directly for the Vera Rubin platform, with operations planned from the turn of the year, and comparable projects are under way in Luleå and Strängnäs.

Source: NVIDIA, CNBC, Fortune, i3 Insights, Digi.no
Debate

92 seconds of AI footage in an NRK documentary — Norwegian creators are furious

The documentary series “Vår verdensarv” premiered on NRK last week. Just 33 seconds into the first episode, viewers meet an AI-generated clip labelled “KI-generert visualisering” — AI-generated visualisation.

According to the trade paper Journalisten, NRK has received at least 16 complaints from the public. Illustrator Flu Hartberg, who leads the campaign group Kiki, calls it a betrayal by public service broadcasting and argues that around ten occupational groups are being passed over. Lene Renneflott, managing director of Grafill, warns that price pressure is dismantling the creative industry, and Elisabeth Sjaastad, president of the Norwegian Film Workers Association, points to paragraph 17 of the NRK charter, which says NRK shall stimulate Norwegian production environments. Director Jonny Westernes at the production company Fabelaktiv responds that the AI clips make up well under one per cent of the series, illustrate only events at least 300 years old, and that a filmed equivalent would have cost tens of thousands of kroner per scene. NRK head of acquisitions Fredrik Færden counts 92 seconds across four hours of running time and concedes it could have been solved with archive footage instead.

For the Nordics this is one of the first boundary tests of the EU transparency requirements, which took effect on August 2 and demand clear labelling of AI-generated content in matters of public interest. In Norway the requirements have no direct legal force yet, because the AI Act has not been incorporated into the EEA Agreement; the government is aiming to put a Norwegian AI bill before parliament in spring 2027 at the earliest, with Nkom as coordinating supervisory authority. The series was correctly labelled — the criticism was about something else, namely whether public service broadcasting should use AI to stay within budget. The same question awaits SVT, KLYS and the Swedish Union of Journalists in Sweden, where the labelling requirements do already apply in full.

Source: VG, Journalisten, Digi.no
Labour market

85 per cent of Swedish industry has not taken AI past the pilot stage

A Nordic survey from the enterprise software vendor IFS, published on August 24, finds that 85 per cent of the Swedish respondents are still exploring AI or running pilot projects. Only 15 per cent have moved on to live operation, and the 40 per cent who use no AI tools at all sit inside that 85.

The obstacle is not lack of interest but data: 51 per cent of the Swedish respondents name data quality and data access as one of the biggest barriers to scaling up. Supply chain optimisation ranks highest as a value area at 26 per cent, followed by predictive maintenance. On a five-point scale, Swedish respondents rate their own organisation’s ability to adopt AI fast enough at 3.53, close to the Nordic average of 3.55. The figures should be read with care: they rest on a total of 294 responses from Sweden, Norway, Denmark and Finland, collected interactively during IFS’s own customer event on April 23. How many of the respondents were Swedish is not stated. This is a self-selected sample at a vendor with an interest in the answer — a temperature reading, not statistics on Nordic industry.

For the Nordics the pattern still points to a skills question that rarely makes headlines. What is missing on the shop floor at the suppliers to Volvo, Sandvik, SSAB and Kongsberg is not more models but data engineers, measurement systems and someone who owns the process data — precisely the role that Vinnova, RISE and Sintef are building programmes around, and that Teknikföretagen and NHO keep returning to in their skills forecasts. IFS Nordic head Mattias Bolander puts it as companies not lacking ambition, but lacking the link between AI and operational reality.

Source: Nordiska Projekt, IFS
Models

Claude gets a single memory for chat and Cowork — on by default

From August 25, Anthropic’s Claude shares one and the same memory between chat and the task tool Cowork. What the model learned in a conversation is there when it runs a task in Cowork, and the other way around.

Everything stored is listed as short files by topic under settings, where each entry can be read, edited or deleted — correct an outdated company name once and it carries through both in chat and in tasks that are run. Sensitive topics such as health, ethnicity, religion, politics and gender identity stay out of memory unless the user actively allows it, and the service does not store personal identity numbers, criminal history or immigration status at all. Memory is on by default for the consumer plans Free, Pro and Max on web, desktop and mobile, but off by default for Team and Enterprise, where the administrator decides.

For the Nordics it is that distinction that determines what employers need to do. An employee who runs Cowork on a personal account against customer data starts processing personal data without a data processing agreement. Under the GDPR the responsibility for it sits with the employer — not with the employee and not with the vendor — and supervision is exercised by the Swedish Authority for Privacy Protection and by Datatilsynet in Norway. Since Norway has no AI act of its own until spring 2027 at the earliest, the GDPR remains for now the only sharp instrument in both Oslo and Stockholm.

Source: Anthropic, Claude Support

Debate

Anthropic puts USD 5 million into independent tests of how AI affects wellbeing

On August 25, Anthropic opened a USD 5 million grant programme for researchers building open evaluations of how language models affect the wellbeing of the people who use them. Applications close on September 21.

The money comes with model access and technical support, but grantees work entirely independently and must publish everything as open source so that any developer can build on it. Applicants invited to submit full proposals will be notified by October 5. Anthropic’s safeguards team is publishing guidance alongside the programme on what makes a wellbeing evaluation robust enough to rely on, and which common weaknesses limit an evaluation’s usefulness. The company states plainly that the industry still lacks clear standards for how a model should behave when someone starts seeking companionship from it, or uses it to work through a mental health crisis.

For the Nordics this lands in the middle of decisions already being made. The Norwegian government has issued a national recommendation that pupils in year groups 1 to 7 should in the main not have access to generative AI, and the Norwegian Data Protection Authority is carrying out five new on-site inspections this year of how municipalities handle personal data in schools. From August 24, ChatGPT also began showing advertising to Swedish and Norwegian users on the free and Go plans, which makes the question of what shapes a model’s answers in a sensitive conversation something other than academic. Open evaluations would give regulators such as the Swedish Authority for Privacy Protection and the Norwegian Data Protection Authority, and researchers at Karolinska Institutet, something to lean on other than the vendor’s own numbers.

Source: Anthropic, Regjeringen.no, Datatilsynet
Models

A free model with a million-token context is drawing developers, and nobody knows who runs it

A model called Ox Alpha appeared on the routing service OpenRouter on August 20 under the provider name Stealth. It is free, carries a context window of 1,048,576 tokens, and no one has claimed it.

The model accepts text, images and video and is marketed for coding, long-running agent work and production use, the same ground Claude, GPT-5.6 and the large Chinese open models are fighting over. The coding agent OpenCode, which offered near-unlimited access during a trial week, says the provider has capacity for 100 trillion tokens of inference a day. Accounts of data retention diverge: OpenRouter’s own listing says prompts are retained by the provider but not used for training, while OpenCode advertises zero retention. Stripe chief executive Patrick Collison called the model very impressive after testing it. The leading theory points to Z.ai, which has tested models anonymously before, while an analysis of the tokeniser points instead to Microsoft’s MAI family.

For the Nordics the problem is the anonymity, not the price. Obligations for general-purpose models under the EU AI Act have applied since August 2, 2025, and since August 2 this year the EU AI Office has been able to investigate and fine providers that fail to meet them. A provider without a name cannot become an identifiable data processor either, and without a processing agreement there is no route for Swedish government agencies or Norwegian municipalities to feed in real content: prompts retained by an unknown party cannot be reconciled with the requirements of either the Swedish or the Norwegian data protection authority. It is the same reasoning that led Helse Sor-Ost to choose a solution running in Norwegian data centres under Norwegian law when the region bought AI-driven clinical documentation for more than 72,000 users.

Source: The Next Web, OpenRouter, Winbuzzer
Quantum

Japan brings its first quantum computer built on neutral atoms into operation

Japan’s Institute for Molecular Science announced on August 24 that the quantum computer Shunkai is operational. It is the country’s first full-stack system built on neutral atoms, with 50 qubits today and a target of 10,000 physical qubits by March 2031.

The system was built by the institute together with Hitachi, which supplied the software stack, and the American company Infleqtion, which contributed the quantum processing unit and is the only foreign firm in Japan’s Moonshot quantum programme. Full stack means that everything between the user’s code and the individual atoms is joined into a single programmable instrument. The atoms are held in place by optical tweezers, tightly focused laser beams, and can be moved between operations, which allows any two qubits to interact and suits the error-correcting codes the field is betting on. At 50 qubits Shunkai is still a research instrument: error correction requires hundreds of physical qubits per logical qubit.

For the Nordics the connection runs through the optics. NKT Photonics in Denmark, part of the Hamamatsu Photonics group, signed a memorandum of understanding on June 3 together with its parent company and Japan’s Yaqumo to industrialise precisely the optical components that atom-based machines need, with NKT supplying the fibre laser systems. The signing took place at the Danish ambassador’s residence in Tokyo, and Yaqumo was founded by, among others, Professor Kenji Ohmori, who leads the Shunkai programme. Chalmers and the Wallenberg Centre for Quantum Technology, together with Finland’s IQM, have instead bet on superconducting qubits, the architecture neutral atoms are now challenging. If the market moves toward atoms rather than cooled circuits, Danish fibre optics ends up in the middle of the supply chain while the Swedish and Finnish lead sits in a different technology.

Source: PR Newswire / IMS, Quantum Computing Report, The Quantum Insider
Regulation

Chinese lab holds back open model weights after high cyber capability scores

Zhipu AI, which trades internationally as Z.ai, launched the coding model GLM-5.3 on August 14 but held back the downloadable weights. The company says they will be published around August 28, the first time Zhipu has delayed a weight release.

The reason is in the model’s own numbers. By the company’s own measurement GLM-5.3 scored 84.5 percent on CyberGym, a benchmark for how well a model finds known vulnerabilities in source code, up from 77.2 percent for its predecessor GLM-5.2. The same measurement puts Anthropic’s Mythos 5 at 83.8 percent and GPT-5.6 Sol at 83.6 percent. On ExploitBench, which measures the ability to actually exploit a flaw, GLM-5.3 stops at 54.4 percent against 78.0 for Mythos 5, so the model is markedly better at finding vulnerabilities than at getting through them. The company’s public disclosure ledger credits the GLM models with 2,436 findings across 269 open source projects, of which 1,097 were rated critical or high severity.

For the Nordics the timing is what matters. Since August 2 the EU AI Office has been able to investigate and fine providers of general-purpose models, with penalties of up to the higher of EUR 15 million and 3 percent of global turnover, and the systemic risk assessment requirement applies whether or not the weights are open. At the same time, open weights are the only way for Norway’s National Security Authority, the Swedish Civil Contingencies Agency and Nordic banks to run a model entirely within their own walls. If GLM-5.3 is released on August 28, CERT-SE and Norway’s NorCERT gain a tool that by the maker’s own tests finds vulnerabilities better than most closed alternatives, available to defenders and attackers alike.

Source: BetaNews, Implicator.ai, European Commission

Hardware

Nvidia raises AI server prices by more than 15 percent

Nvidia has told its largest customers that servers built around the company’s AI chips will cost more than 15 percent more. The increase applies to systems shipping in early 2027.

Bloomberg reported the move on August 22, and it covers the Vera Rubin and Grace Blackwell platforms. The size of the increase varies with chip generation and with how much memory a given configuration carries. The cause is memory pricing: the Rubin package ships with up to 288 GB of HBM4, and HBM requires considerably more silicon area than the same amount of conventional DRAM. The server builders that supply Microsoft, Google and Oracle have passed the notice on to their own customers. Semiconductor stocks fell on Monday, August 24, in a broader technology sell-off — Micron by 5.8 percent and AMD by more than 3 percent — two days before Nvidia reports quarterly results on August 26.

For the Nordics this is partly a pension question. Nvidia is the single largest equity holding of Norway’s Government Pension Fund Global: a 1.3 percent stake worth USD 61.8 billion as of June 30. The fund returned 9.4 percent in the first half of the year and was worth NOK 22,683 billion at the half-year mark, and seven of its ten largest absolute value increases were in semiconductor and semiconductor equipment companies. The AI data centres now being built in northern Sweden and in Norway buy exactly the same hardware — if racks cost 15 percent more, the bill rises for every new site. The August 26 report is therefore a message to both Norges Bank Investment Management and Nordic data centre developers.

Source: Bloomberg, Tom’s Hardware, CNBC
Physical AI

Saab unveils uncrewed fighter designed to fly ahead of the Gripen E

On August 21 Saab presented a full-scale model of the A3-001, an uncrewed combat aircraft intended to operate alongside the JAS 39 Gripen E. The unveiling came ahead of the Jubilee Air Show at Malmen Air Base in Linköping on August 22 and 23, the conclusion of the Swedish Air Force’s centenary celebrations.

Peter Nilsson, Head of Advanced Programs at Saab, said the company will first build a demonstrator designated A1 to inform the later A2 and A3 steps. The model on display is the A3 configuration, without a vertical stabiliser, though Saab will test prototypes both with and without one. Together with the Swedish Air Force and the Defence Materiel Administration (FMV), the company is aiming for the demonstrator to fly within a year and a half, meaning late 2027 or early 2028, while display material for the finished aircraft points to a first flight in 2031 and operational capability in 2035. The intended missions are electronic warfare, suppression of enemy air defences and precision strike in heavily defended areas. The engine has not been chosen — Nilsson points to the same engine as the Gripen E, the GKN Aerospace RM16, the Swedish version of the General Electric F414 — and the aircraft is meant to be considerably cheaper than the Gripen.

For the Nordics the question is how much decision-making moves into software. Saab has just begun delivering the Gripen E and says it intends to fly uncrewed demonstrators with fighter-like characteristics before 2030 — a signal that lands with the Swedish Armed Forces and FMV as the next generation of combat air systems is chosen. On the Norwegian side, Kongsberg is developing autonomous weapon systems in the same direction, while Norway has already committed to the F-35. The question of who carries responsibility when an uncrewed aircraft makes its own decisions in the air will therefore reach both the Riksdag and the Storting sooner or later.

Source: Saab, The Aviationist, Corren
Infrastructure

Equinor places 13 years of measurement technology with Emerson

At the ONS energy conference in Stavanger on August 24, Equinor selected Emerson to supply measurement instrumentation, analytical technology and lifecycle services across its offshore and onshore operations. The frame agreement runs for up to 13 years including options.

The agreement extends the two companies’ ongoing digitalisation and automation work and is tied to Equinor’s NCS 2035 initiative, which aims to sustain production and improve recovery on the Norwegian continental shelf. Emerson has supplied technology to Equinor for more than four decades. The background is Equinor’s statement in January that AI saved the company USD 130 million during 2025, mainly through predictive maintenance and machine learning applied to seismic data interpretation.

For the Nordics the point is that models never get better than the instruments feeding them. A 13-year measurement technology agreement is in practice a decision about the data quality the Norwegian shelf will have in the 2030s, and that is the data Equinor’s AI models will read. In the same week Equinor, Aker BP and Vår Energi presented a multi-year exploration partnership on the shelf in which data and analytical capacity are to be shared. For SINTEF, NTNU and the Norwegian supplier industry, that turns the sensor–model–decision chain into an export product — the same pattern ABB and Kongsberg already sell into other process industries.

Source: World Oil, Offshore Magazine, E24
Research

Researchers: state procurement is locking the AI market into a handful of suppliers

Two economists warn in Nature on August 24 that government procurement is concentrating the markets for AI, quantum technology and space technology in a small number of suppliers. The lock-in happens before the technologies have had time to mature.

Luisa Corrado of Tor Vergata University in Rome and Paul Kattuman of Cambridge Judge Business School point out that the US federal AI contracts the article maps for 2025 went to just four companies, and that IBM took roughly half of the planned US quantum computing funding for 2026 according to the same review. In the space sector, SpaceX accounted for 118 launches, 83 percent of all FAA-licensed launches, in fiscal year 2024. The United Kingdom’s national quantum strategy allocates GBP 2.5 billion over ten years from 2024. The authors propose copying DARPA’s three-stage model from the Quantum Benchmarking Initiative, in which several suppliers advance in parallel and are filtered on measurable criteria rather than a winner being picked early.

For the Nordics the problem is larger than in the United States, not smaller.
