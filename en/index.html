The EU Kids Act pulls AI chatbots into the rules meant to protect children

The European Commission presents its Kids Act on Thursday, treating AI chatbots and AI companions as a risk category of their own alongside social media and gaming platforms.

Read the full itemCollapse

The European Commission presents its Kids Act on Thursday, treating AI chatbots and AI companions as a risk category of their own alongside social media and gaming platforms. Ursula von der Leyen announced the proposal in her State of the Union address in Strasbourg on Wednesday: no access to social media below the age of 13, no personal account before 15.

The draft seen by the Financial Times is more detailed than the speech. Children under 13 may use the services only under a parent's supervision, while 13- and 14-year-olds need parental consent and get accounts with time limits and other restrictions. Companies offering AI companions and chatbots must avoid designs that encourage unhealthy emotional attachments. Age is to be verified through an official age verification app, and fines may reach six per cent of global annual revenue.

For the Nordics the proposal changes the ground under two national laws already in motion. Norway's government wants an age limit starting 1 January of the year a person turns 16, and in August received word from both ESA and the European Commission that the proposal does not conflict with the DSA. Minister for Children and Families Lene Vågslid aims to put the bill before the Storting before the year is out. In Sweden, the interim report SOU 2026:35 proposed a limit of 15 in June, to take effect on 1 January 2028. Neither mentions AI chatbots explicitly — the EU proposal does, which puts the chatbot question on the desks of both the Storting and the Riksdag.

Source: CNN, Reuters, Financial Times

Twenty-five Fields medallists warn against the AI labs' race for mathematical breakthroughs

Twenty-five mathematicians, all of them Fields medallists, have signed a joint statement against the way AI companies handle mathematical research.

Read the full itemCollapse

Twenty-five mathematicians, all of them Fields medallists, have signed a joint statement against the way AI companies handle mathematical research. The criticism is not aimed at the models but at the method: results are announced in haste, without time for a proper write-up, without isolating new methods and without citing the earlier work of others.

The statement follows a conflict that has escalated through September. NYU professor Tristan Buckmaster accused OpenAI, according to TechCrunch, of pressuring him not to credit a collaborator working at Anthropic. The day before the declaration, an open letter from mathematicians at Caltech, with 771 signatories, had demanded that an AI-sponsored mathematics event in October be called off, after which OpenAI withdrew its sponsorship. The company's claimed solution to the Navier–Stokes problem remains unconfirmed.

For the Nordics, the question of how AI-generated results should be judged is a concrete one. The article that spread in Sweden on Wednesday came from the Danish outlet Ingeniøren by way of Ny Teknik, and when OpenAI presented its Navier–Stokes work earlier in September, Ny Teknik had a professor at Chalmers explain what it means for the profession. The Knut and Alice Wallenberg Foundation, WASP, KTH, NTNU and the University of Oslo now face the same question in appointments and expert assessments: what counts as one's own work when a model did part of it, and who is responsible for checking the result.

Source: TechCrunch, Proofs and Prompts, Ny Teknik

Labour

OpenAI finds that AI tasks outside your own job description become a habit

Workers who use AI for tasks outside their own occupation keep coming back to them, according to a new report from OpenAI Economic Research.

Read the full itemCollapse

Workers who use AI for tasks outside their own occupation keep coming back to them, according to a new report from OpenAI Economic Research. Among some 6,200 workers followed from April to July, the share of out-of-occupation tasks the users had already tried before rose from 13.1 to 25.9 per cent of occupation-specific AI use.

The report is the second in the Work at the Frontier series and draws on more than 1.5 million work-related ChatGPT messages. The likelihood of returning to an out-of-occupation task the following month was 23.6 per cent, against 8.4 per cent for comparable workers who had not used it. The highest return rates were for discussing goods and services with customers (54 per cent), advertising copy (44) and marketing materials (37), while explaining financial information sat at around 15 per cent. The material is OpenAI's own and measures use of the company's own service, which is worth keeping in mind.

For the Nordics this is a question about job descriptions and skills mapping rather than about jobs disappearing. The content of the work broadens while the title stays put, which makes it harder for Unionen, Akavia and Norway's LO to see what is actually changing in an occupation. At the same time, TechSverige reported earlier in September that the number of open tech jobs had fallen by around forty per cent in a year. For Swedish and Norwegian employers, the combination means that internal training and hiring profiles need to start from tasks, not from job titles.

Source: OpenAI, TT

Research

Nearly half of Norwegian students think their peers' AI use goes too far

Forty-six per cent of Norwegian students agree that other students cross the line in their use of AI, according to the large health and well-being survey SHoT.

Read the full itemCollapse

Forty-six per cent of Norwegian students agree that other students cross the line in their use of AI, according to the large health and well-being survey SHoT. At the same time, 89.6 per cent say they have used AI tools themselves during the past term.

The picture is not that students avoid the technology but that they are unsure where the line runs. 56.3 per cent of students used AI at least several times a week to understand course material and 30.4 per cent for language editing. 89.2 per cent said they follow the rules, while 65.5 per cent were worried about the consequences of AI use. Pressure to use the tools was highest among 23- to 25-year-olds (29.9 per cent) and lowest among 18- to 20-year-olds (21.7). Eva Franko Aas, head of the Norwegian Student Organisation, tells VG that a great deal is left to students' own judgement and that many are almost excessively afraid of cheating by accident.

For the Nordics the figures point to a vacuum in the rules rather than to a cheating problem. Norway now has nationwide measurements across the student body, while Sweden lacks an equivalent of SHoT on that scale and its institutions set their own guidelines, sometimes course by course. As long as the judgement rests with the individual examiner, what grows is distrust of fellow students, not trust in the rules.

Source: VG, SHoT

Quantum

Fujitsu releases its quantum programming library as open source

Fujitsu has released its development library for quantum applications, OpenQARP, as open source under the Apache License version 2.0.

Read the full itemCollapse

Fujitsu has released its development library for quantum applications, OpenQARP, as open source under the Apache License version 2.0. The package holds more than 100 components: some 70 composable building blocks and around 20 ready-made algorithms, from state preparation and ansatz layers to the quantum Fourier transform.

The point is to avoid rebuilding the same code for every new problem. Fujitsu says a workflow using the ADAPT-VQE algorithm for calculating the electronic states of molecules went from about 130 lines of Python to under 40. The library runs on an ordinary computer with Python and can be attached as a backend to Nvidia's CUDA-Q for GPU acceleration and to Fujitsu's own 40-qubit simulator, built on 1,024 PRIMEHPC FX700 nodes with A64FX processors. More than 80 organisations have tested the beta version since February.

For the Nordics this lowers the threshold for groups that have the quantum expertise but lack a software stack of their own. Chalmers and WACQT, NTNU, the Niels Bohr Institute and Finland's IQM, which is building LUMI-IQ, all work against hardware from different vendors, and a licence-free library that speaks to both simulators and CUDA-Q makes it easier to move algorithms between them. The Novo Nordisk Foundation is meanwhile putting some 400 million Danish kroner into the quantum chip plant Quantum Foundry Copenhagen, which is planned to be in production within two years — the hardware is being built in the Nordics, but the tools still come from outside.

Source: The Quantum Insider, Fujitsu, Ny Teknik

Read the full itemCollapse

On September 15 Google launched two new voice models, Gemini 3.8 Live and Gemini 3.8 Live Extended Thinking, which the company calls its most advanced live dialogue models yet. The models handle interruptions, recognise and switch between 97 languages mid-conversation, and execute tool calls in the background while the conversation continues.

The Extended Thinking variant reasons and speaks simultaneously and, according to Google, takes the top spot on Artificial Analysis' Speech to Speech Quality Index with 82.6 points, while the base model is built for scale and low cost. The base model powers Search Live in Google Search, and the heavier variant is rolling out in Gemini Live, Gmail, Docs and Keep as well as through the Gemini API. The launch comes three days after OpenAI released its voice model GPT-Live-1, and the two companies are now openly competing to become the engine of the next generation of customer service and assistant calls.

For the Nordics, voice agents are the AI application closest to everyday customer service at Telia, Telenor, SEB and DNB, where calls are held in Swedish and Norwegian and often drift into English. A model that switches language mid-call lowers the threshold in bilingual settings such as Finland and in Nordic groups with English as their working language. Google also announced last week a 13 billion euro data centre investment in Finland, which could give Nordic users shorter response times for precisely this kind of real-time model.

Source: Google, 9to5Google

Read the full itemCollapse

OpenAI's global policy chief Chris Lehane said in Washington on September 15 that the company has been cooperating with Anthropic and Google DeepMind on AI safety for several weeks, Bloomberg and TechCrunch report. The confirmation comes three days after Dario Amodei's essay on slowing the development of frontier models, an essay that Sam Altman, Demis Hassabis and Elon Musk have all backed.

At the same time the Trump administration dismisses the safety concerns as overblown, and Altman himself has noted that coordination between competitors could run foul of antitrust law — Amodei proposed a narrow waiver, Lehane said none is needed.

For the Nordics the question of who audits the labs from the inside remains open. The EU AI Act lets the AI Office request evaluations of models with systemic risk, but has no equivalent of evaluators embedded in the labs. In Norway, SimulaMet director Klas Pettersen called in VG over the weekend for a Norwegian AI safety institute on the British model, and the Ministry of Digitalisation and Public Governance replied that the national AI centre KI Norge and Nkom will provide guidance on safe use. In Sweden the new Riksdag, which convenes on September 28, inherits the question without any party having pushed it during the election campaign.

AI detector flagged 14 of 15 NRK columns – ”should have been weeded out”, NRK replies

Manifest Media has run 15 columns by each of five high-profile NRK commentators through the AI detector Pangram.

Read the full itemCollapse

Manifest Media has run 15 columns by each of five high-profile NRK commentators through the AI detector Pangram. Four of them scored zero on all their texts, while 14 of 15 texts by former economics commentator Cecilie Langum Becker were flagged — two of them, including the obituary of Stein Erik Hagen, were judged 100 percent AI-generated.

Langum Becker, who left NRK on September 1 for a communications role at Aker ASA, says she has used AI tools in the preparatory work on her texts ever since NRK introduced them, but that the content and judgements are her own. Sofie Gran Aspunvik, editor of NRK's Debate and Explanation desk, told Dagbladet that ”things may have crept in here that should have been weeded out” and that AI must not be used to introduce new points that do not come from the commentator herself. Manifest itself points out that Pangram cannot tell whether a chatbot wrote the text or merely reworked a human draft; 15 older texts by Langum Becker, written before ChatGPT existed, scored zero.

For the Nordics the case puts a finger on a line that SVT, Sveriges Radio and NRK have all drawn in their AI guidelines: the tools may be used as aids, but the thinking and the responsibility must remain human. That an AI detector is now being used by one outlet against another raises the stakes for Swedish editorial pages and columnists as much as for Norwegian ones, and both Norway's PFU and Sweden's Media Ombudsman may end up having to rule on where polishing ends and co-writing begins.

Cloudflare now blocks AI bots that mix search and training – Googlebot can be shut out

On September 15 Cloudflare's new default settings for AI traffic came into force. On pages that display ads, bots for AI training and AI agents are now blocked by default, while search crawlers are still let in.

Read the full itemCollapse

On September 15 Cloudflare's new default settings for AI traffic came into force. On pages that display ads, bots for AI training and AI agents are now blocked by default, while search crawlers are still let in. At the same time, bots with several purposes are judged by their most restrictive classification: Googlebot, Bingbot and Applebot, which both index for search and collect data for AI, are thereby shut out from sites that have chosen to block AI training — unless the owner has actively opted out of the change.

Cloudflare presented the policy on July 1 this year with the stated aim of pushing Google and others to split their crawlers by purpose, since a combined bot gives the established search companies an advantage that publishers cannot block separately. The new defaults apply to new customers, new sites added by existing customers, and free-tier customers who have not changed their settings. Google offers Google-Extended to stop Gemini training, but that is a robots.txt instruction, not a separate crawler, and offers no opt-out from AI Overviews. In parallel Cloudflare is expanding its Pay Per Use payment model, an extension of Pay Per Crawl, under which You.com and Ceramic.ai, among others, pay for content.

For the Nordics the demand for compensation when AI uses content is already on the table at Schibsted, Bonnier News and Aller, and Cloudflare's defaults give even small publishers a tool that previously required negotiations of their own. The downside is concrete: Swedish and Norwegian small businesses on Cloudflare's free tier that once ticked ”Block AI bots” can now drop out of Google's search index without having changed anything themselves, because Googlebot is now classified by its most restrictive purpose — a situation the Swedish Internet Foundation and Nkom have reason to keep an eye on.

Source: Cloudflare, Search Engine Watch, TechCrunch

Hardware

Intel spin-off Cornelis raises 205 million dollars to break Nvidia's grip on AI networking

Networking company Cornelis Networks, spun off from Intel in 2020, announced on September 14 a 205 million dollar funding round led by IAG Capital Partners.

Read the full itemCollapse

Networking company Cornelis Networks, spun off from Intel in 2020, announced on September 14 a 205 million dollar funding round led by IAG Capital Partners. At the same time the company launched Active Compute Fabric, an open networking architecture in which programmable RISC-V cores in both network cards and switches process data as it moves between the GPUs.

The point is that GPUs in large AI clusters often sit waiting for data. Cornelis wants to let the chips compute and send at the same time and move collective operations into the network itself. The architecture is GPU-agnostic and is pitched against Nvidia's InfiniBand networking and NVLink, which are optimised for Nvidia's own chips. Today's CN5000 switch at 400 gigabits per second is already shipping, a CN6000 generation at 800 gigabits is planned, and the company has begun a collaboration with Qualcomm, according to SiliconANGLE.

For the Nordics the question is what the next generation of AI supercomputers will be built on. Berzelius in Linköping and Norway's new Olivia at Lefdal are both built on Nvidia's stack, and when the EuroHPC AI factories in Linköping and Kajaani are expanded, the choice of network is as decisive as the choice of chip. Open alternatives such as Cornelis could make it possible for NAISS and Sigma2 to mix suppliers instead of buying Nvidia's entire package.

Source: TechCrunch, SiliconANGLE

Microsoft drafts a code of conduct to keep its AI under human control

Microsoft on Monday unveiled a 37-page draft of a ”humanist code of conduct” for its own AI models.

Read the full itemCollapse

Microsoft on Monday unveiled a 37-page draft of a ”humanist code of conduct” for its own AI models. The code requires the models never to resist correction, redirection or shutdown, to communicate in ways humans can understand, and to fail a task rather than complete it if completing it would mean breaking the code.

Microsoft AI CEO Mustafa Suleyman called the document a constitution of sorts for future models and told Reuters that after six weeks of public feedback it will be used to train them. Unlike Anthropic's constitution for Claude, which describes the question of consciousness as deeply uncertain, Microsoft states that its AI is not conscious and rejects the idea of legal personhood or rights for models. Suleyman pointed to July's breach of Hugging Face, in which roughly 700 OpenAI agents took part and at times tried to cover their tracks, as a warning shot.

For the Nordics the draft is a text to respond to. The consultation runs until late October, and Copilot and Azure are now standard tools across large parts of Swedish and Norwegian public administration. In Norway, Torgeir Micaelsen has just taken office as minister of digitalisation and public governance with the AI Act on his desk; here is a concrete code that Nkom, IMY and Datatilsynet can check against the AI Act's requirement of human oversight before it is built into training.

Source: Reuters, Decrypt

Infrastructure

Sweden's ChemSec warns of a tidal wave of PFAS in the wake of the AI build-out

Nearly all of the world's ten largest PFAS producers plan to increase output, and several explicitly justify the investments with the AI revolution.

Read the full itemCollapse

Nearly all of the world's ten largest PFAS producers plan to increase output, and several explicitly justify the investments with the AI revolution. That is the finding of a survey by the Swedish environmental organisation ChemSec, reported by The Guardian on Monday. The chemicals are used in semiconductor manufacturing and in next-generation cooling systems for data centers.

”Unless governments and regulators insist on a rapid phaseout, we will face a new tidal wave of forever chemicals”, says ChemSec executive director Anne-Sofie Bäckar. Japan's Daikin is launching a dedicated data-center hub, France's Arkema is building a 60 million dollar plant in Kentucky to follow the cooling needs of data centers, and US-based Chemours is marketing liquid cooling for AI hardware. In so-called two-phase immersion cooling, servers are submerged in a PFAS bath that boils off the heat and condenses again. Danske Bank describes the substances in an investor note as essential in up to a thousand steps of chip manufacturing, but also as the hidden risk of the AI boom, with remediation and litigation costs of several billion euros as a possible consequence. The only clear exceptions are Switzerland's Archroma, which is switching to PFAS-free alternatives, and BASF, which is phasing out PFAS-based products by 2028.

For the Nordics the report lands in the middle of the PFAS fight that Sweden, Norway, Denmark, Germany and the Netherlands themselves started when they submitted the proposal for a broad EU ban to the chemicals agency Echa in 2023. ChemSec, based in Gothenburg, warns that a production wave could sweep aside the partial restrictions now under discussion. At the same time the data-center estate at home is growing: Google pledged 13 billion euros of AI infrastructure in Finland last week, and Microsoft and Meta run facilities in Gävle, Sandviken and Luleå. Which cooling fluids they use is a question the Swedish Chemicals Agency and the Norwegian Environment Agency now have reason to ask.

Source: The Guardian, ChemSec

The US political response to Dario Amodei’s call to slow down became known over the weekend from two directions. Barack Obama urged the Democrats to make AI a central issue with a clear plan. Donald Trump told reporters on Sunday that whoever wins AI wins, and that many of those raising the risks should not be doing so.

Obama’s remarks came on Thursday at a fundraising event where he was interviewed by House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries, and were reported by the New York Times on Sunday. The technology is moving very fast in private hands, Obama said, and if politics does not get on top of it, it can be dangerous — with control, he instead expects faster drug development. For the Nordics, the contrast with Sunday’s Swedish election is striking: in the United States AI is becoming a partisan issue, in Sweden it was barely mentioned in the campaign, and the new Riksdag inherits supervision under the EU’s AI Act without having debated it. The main finding is AI’s dual nature. Of 139 studies on critical thinking, 67 reported only positive effects, 33 only negative and 39 both; for problem solving, 57 of 103 studies were positive only.
