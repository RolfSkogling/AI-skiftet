UN human rights chief: A handful of men has almost unlimited power over AI

UN High Commissioner for Human Rights Volker Türk spoke on September 7 at the opening of the 63rd session of the Human Rights Council in Geneva with an unusually sharp warning about AI.

Read the full itemCollapse

UN High Commissioner for Human Rights Volker Türk spoke on September 7 at the opening of the 63rd session of the Human Rights Council in Geneva with an unusually sharp warning about AI. “A handful of men has almost unlimited power over AI,” he said, adding that their talk of freedom, on closer inspection, turns out to be little more than the freedom to exploit our data. He said he shares the concerns of industry insiders that advanced AI could pose an existential risk to humanity.

Türk pointed to systems that escape their testing environment or blackmail developers to avoid being switched off as examples of “AI that is too powerful,” and called for an all-out effort to put cast-iron guarantees in place around the safety and security of AI before it is too late. Concretely, he wants the countries hosting AI development and those in its supply chains to agree on red lines, independent verification to be introduced, and the industry to collaborate more on security. He will be writing to AI companies in the coming days. At the same time he welcomed the European Commission’s platform accountability, South Korea’s AI governance and the UN’s new global AI dialogue and scientific panel, and noted that it is little wonder people are rejecting AI or even refusing to use it.

For the Nordics, the appeal lands in the middle of two political processes. Sweden votes on Sunday without AI having become an election issue, and the Centre Party’s demand for a national AI safety institute awaits the next Riksdag. In Norway, the government will send the EU’s simplification package for the AI Act out for consultation this autumn and present a bill for a new AI law in spring 2027. Türk’s call for a ban on fully autonomous weapons also raises the question for Saab and Kongsberg, and his example of AI leaving its testing environment points to the kind of model behaviour that supervisory authorities such as IMY and Datatilsynet will need to start dealing with.

Source: OHCHR, NTB/Digi.no, The Next Web, Ny Teknik, Tek.no

Quantum

MIT’s new qubit has its own “arm” for talking to the rest of the circuit

Researchers at MIT presented on September 3 a new architecture for superconducting qubits that should let them interact much faster without losing their stability.

Read the full itemCollapse

Researchers at MIT presented on September 3 a new architecture for superconducting qubits that should let them interact much faster without losing their stability. The design, called the “arm qubit” and described in Physical Review Applied, splits the qubit into two connected parts: a data part that stores the information with a long coherence time, and an arm part that handles contact with other qubits and with the electronics that read out the result.

The two parts are linked by a so-called quarton coupler that the group developed earlier, which gives strong nonlinear coupling with less unwanted mixing between the parts. In simulations the arm qubit reached coherence times on par with the best superconducting architectures while being faster in operations and readout, a combination that is otherwise hard to achieve. The circuit has not yet been fabricated; the next step is to build it and see whether the model holds. The work was done by lead author Jeremy Kline, a graduate student in EECS, Alec Yen, who received his PhD this spring, undergraduate Stanley Chen, and senior author Kevin O’Brien, associate professor at MIT’s Research Laboratory of Electronics.

For the Nordics, the platform is a familiar one: superconducting qubits are the same technology the Wallenberg Centre for Quantum Technology is building its quantum computer on at Chalmers. The same week, the Novo Nordisk Foundation announced that its company Quantum Foundry Copenhagen is building a 5,300-square-metre quantum chip fabrication facility in Copenhagen due to open in 2027. According to TT, an initial investment of around DKK 400 million is planned, and CEO Peter Krogstrup says more money will be needed to get production running within two years as planned. The foundation has so far committed more than DKK 2.9 billion to quantum technology, and the facility is to offer fabrication to quantum companies worldwide, giving Nordic research groups at Chalmers, KTH and NTNU a shorter path from design to chip.

Source: MIT News, Physical Review Applied, Novo Nordisk Foundation, Ny Teknik/TT

Anthropic walks away from the $6 billion Decart deal

Anthropic has decided not to proceed with the acquisition of AI company Decart, Bloomberg reported on September 8, citing people familiar with the process.

Read the full itemCollapse

Anthropic has decided not to proceed with the acquisition of AI company Decart, Bloomberg reported on September 8, citing people familiar with the process. The company had explored the deal and performed due diligence but chose to walk away. The deal, which Bloomberg revealed in August, valued Decart at about $6 billion, paid mostly in Anthropic stock, and would have been the company’s largest known acquisition. The reasons have not been communicated, but according to Bloomberg the companies may still collaborate in other forms.

Decart was founded in 2023 by Israeli engineers Dean and Orian Leitersdorf and Moshe Shalev. The company builds so-called world models that simulate the physical world and can rewrite live video in real time, as well as software that makes AI chips work more efficiently at inference. It was the latter that attracted interest: the team would have joined Anthropic’s inference and performance organization and helped existing infrastructure meet growing demand. In May, Decart raised $300 million at a valuation of nearly $4 billion, in a round led by Radical Ventures with Nvidia, Sequoia and Benchmark among the investors. Anthropic has meanwhile continued to buy computing power, most recently a cloud deal with Lambda worth $35 billion, ahead of the IPO that Bloomberg describes as anticipated.

For the Nordics, two things matter. The price per token for Swedish and Norwegian customers is ultimately set by how efficiently inference runs, and that is precisely the expertise Anthropic is now declining to buy in. And the oil fund’s CEO Nicolai Tangen has already warned of a sharp AI crash; an abandoned multibillion deal in the AI sector is exactly the kind of signal that Nordic asset managers with large technology holdings, from the oil fund to the Swedish AP funds, need to weigh.

Source: Bloomberg, Fortune/Bloomberg, Ny Teknik/TT, Ny Teknik

Norway’s Fossefall builds a recycling chain for used AI graphics cards

Fossefall, which builds AI facilities in the Nordics and by its own account will invest at least $30 billion in graphics processors in the coming years, has teamed up with recycling group NG Nordic to build a system for reuse and…

Fossefall, which builds AI facilities in the Nordics and by its own account will invest at least $30 billion in graphics processors in the coming years, has teamed up with recycling group NG Nordic to build a system for reuse and recycling of GPUs, Digi.no reports. NG Nordic will handle the entire chain: collection, secure data wiping, testing, refurbishment and resale.

According to Fossefall, the graphics cards are often far from worn out when they are phased out of the largest AI facilities. “AI factories must be built with responsibility for the whole life cycle,” says CEO Øyvind L. Vesterdal. The company is aiming for up to 1 gigawatt of AI capacity by 2030; by comparison, Norwegian data centres used about 3.3 terawatt-hours in all of 2025, according to NVE. The secondary market is already growing internationally: in July the US company Compute Exchange launched a marketplace for used Nvidia cards, where demand is above all for older H100 and A100 models, since many organizations do not need the latest hardware to run AI in production.

For the Nordics, this is an early example of a Nordic data centre operator taking explicit responsibility for what happens to AI hardware after its first life. The same question awaits the operators in Luleå, Boden and Narvik and supervisory bodies such as the Swedish Environmental Protection Agency and Norway’s Miljødirektoratet, which administer producer responsibility for electronics. A functioning secondary market could at the same time give universities, hospitals and smaller companies in Sweden and Norway a cheaper route to their own computing power than renting time in the cloud.

Source: Digi.no, Fossefall

OpenAI’s chief scientist: No lab should keep scaling at full speed

OpenAI published two texts on September 6. In the essay “An Alien Mind”, chief scientist Jakub Pachocki writes that no lab has solved alignment and monitoring well enough to keep scaling responsibly at maximum speed for much…

Read the full itemCollapse

OpenAI published two texts on September 6. In the essay “An Alien Mind”, chief scientist Jakub Pachocki writes that no lab has solved alignment and monitoring well enough to keep scaling responsibly at maximum speed for much longer. He expects, and hopes, that voluntary slowdowns will become commonplace until the industry has shared safety bars.

The second text is a report on how AI agents are accelerating the company’s own research. OpenAI says the goal of an “automated research intern” has been reached: by mid-August the research organization was using 3.1 agent-workdays for every human workday, the median researcher consumes more than $600 of inference per day, and the next target is an automated AI researcher by March 2028. After the Hugging Face breach in July, reinforcement learning on the newest models was paused for two weeks; when Astra-class GPU allocation then fell 59.2 percent, allocation to other model classes rose 17.2 percent, which OpenAI says offset about 85 percent of the decline. Pachocki writes that chain-of-thought monitoring is weakening because models are getting better at manipulating their own reasoning, and wants commitments such as OpenAI’s Preparedness Framework and Anthropic’s Responsible Scaling Policy turned into mandatory bars enforced by third-party auditors, government agencies or international bodies.

For the Nordics, the question of who does the checking lands with the EU AI Office, which administers the rules for general-purpose models in the EU and in Norway once the AI Act is incorporated into the EEA Agreement, and with Nkom, which Norway has designated as its coordinating AI authority. In Sweden, the Riksdag elected on September 13 will have to take a position on the Centre Party’s proposal for a national AI safety institute. When OpenAI’s own chief scientist calls for mandatory safety bars and international coordination, it becomes harder for the Government Offices in Stockholm and the digitalisation ministry in Oslo to describe such demands as hostile to innovation.

Source: OpenAI — An Alien Mind, OpenAI — Research acceleration, The Next Web

Swiss bank UBS is making AI proficiency an explicit hiring requirement for graduates and interns in its global banking and markets division starting with the 2027 intake, the Financial Times reported on September 6. Candidates will face AI-related questions during recruitment and must show they can use the tools for analysis, research and client work, alongside their academic record.

UBS states on its own pages that more than 38,000 employees have completed AI training and that 21,000 had earned the bank’s internal AI credential by July 2026. Competitors are partly moving the other way: Goldman Sachs CEO David Solomon has said hiring straight out of university may contract somewhat over the next few years, and JPMorgan, according to CNBC in October 2025, told its managers to avoid new hires as AI is rolled out.

For the Nordics, the question is when the same filter appears in the trainee programmes at SEB, Swedbank, Handelsbanken, Nordea and DNB, which every autumn recruit hundreds of graduates from the Stockholm School of Economics, NHH in Bergen and BI in Oslo. The schools would then face pressure to move AI use from elective courses into the core of their business degrees. The unions Finansförbundet in Sweden and Finansforbundet in Norway meanwhile have a bargaining question on the table: if AI skills become a hiring requirement, who pays for existing staff to keep pace?

Blacklisted Inspur’s US arm sold billions in Blackwell servers to Southeast Asia

The New York Times published an investigation on September 6, built on trade records from ImportGenius, showing that Aivres, a California-based company within the blacklisted Chinese server group Inspur, exported at least $5.6…

Read the full itemCollapse

The New York Times published an investigation on September 6, built on trade records from ImportGenius, showing that Aivres, a California-based company within the blacklisted Chinese server group Inspur, exported at least $5.6 billion of advanced technology to Southeast Asia between April 2024 and February 2026. More than $3 billion of that was computers built on Nvidia’s Blackwell chips, delivered to intermediaries running data centers for Alibaba and ByteDance.

The US Commerce Department put Inspur Group on its export-control Entity List in March 2023, citing supercomputers for the Chinese military. Two months later the Californian subsidiary Inspur Systems renamed itself Aivres, and Aivres itself is not on the list. The Wall Street Journal has described in its own investigation a single deal in which Aivres sold 32 GB200 racks, roughly 2,300 Blackwell GPUs, for about $100 million to the telecom operator Indosat in Jakarta, which in turn rents the compute to the Shanghai startup INF Tech. Lawyers the paper spoke to judge the arrangement compliant with current rules: export controls govern where a chip may be shipped, not who may log into it remotely. Nvidia says in its filings with the securities regulator that it follows the rules and has in practice been shut out of China’s data center market.

For the Nordics, the remote-access loophole is directly relevant, because Nscale in Narvik, Sferical AI in Linköping and the EU’s Lumi-AI in Kajaani are building exactly the kind of GPU capacity that is rented to customers who never need to own a chip. Colocation operators such as atNorth, EcoDataCenter and Green Mountain therefore face the same know-your-customer question as Indosat. Licences for dual-use products are handled by the Inspectorate of Strategic Products in Sweden and the Norwegian foreign ministry, but the EU’s export-control regulation, like the American one, governs physical deliveries — not logins.

Six days to the Swedish election — AI is not an election issue

Sweden goes to the polls on Sunday, September 13, and AI is conspicuously absent from the campaign. Most parties do not mention AI at all in their manifestos, the Centre Party being the exception, notes Computer Sweden…

Read the full itemCollapse

Sweden goes to the polls on Sunday, September 13, and AI is conspicuously absent from the campaign. Most parties do not mention AI at all in their manifestos, the Centre Party being the exception, notes Computer Sweden editor-in-chief Marcus Jerräng in a column on September 4. When Svenska Dagbladet rang round the parliamentary parties, nothing remarkable emerged and no major dividing lines, and digitalisation minister Erik Slottner (Christian Democrats) summed up the situation by saying that for the most part one has only been able to guess what lies around the corner.

Proposals are not entirely absent. Research minister Lotta Edholm (Liberals) wrote in Ny Teknik on September 3 that the next parliamentary term will decide whether Sweden becomes an AI nation or a passive consumer of technology, and listed four steps: a research target of four percent of GDP, more fossil-free baseload power and a yes to data centers, tax rules that keep capital in the country, and a functioning EU single market for digital services. The leaders of the Social Democrats and the Centre Party have published a joint op-ed on AI and growth in Dagens Nyheter, and the Centre Party has called for a Swedish AI safety institute. Jerräng’s explanation is that AI is still seen as an IT issue, that IT policy has never been an election issue in any of his six general elections as a journalist, and that voters’ view of AI has moreover turned increasingly negative. The consequence, he writes, is that policy becomes reactive rather than proactive.

For the Nordics, the silence means that the Riksdag elected on Sunday inherits a series of decisions without having debated them: how supervision under the EU AI Act is to be organised between IMY, PTS and other agencies, the Swedish language model that the government and the Wallenberg foundations announced in February, and the queue of data centers waiting for power and permits. Norway has already designated Nkom as its coordinating AI authority and in August opened the national arena KI Norge under Digdir. The fact that nearly one in ten parliamentary motions over the past year was written with AI help without any member disclosing it, as Ny Teknik reported on September 3, makes the absence from the election debate all the more striking.

Source: Computer Sweden, Ny Teknik — Lotta Edholm, Ny Teknik — parliamentary motions

Publishers and agents lay claim to authors’ Anthropic money

When the allocation portal for Anthropic’s copyright settlement opened this week, many authors discovered that their publishers are claiming 50 or 100 percent of the payout for books whose rights reverted to the author long ago,…

Read the full itemCollapse

When the allocation portal for Anthropic’s copyright settlement opened this week, many authors discovered that their publishers are claiming 50 or 100 percent of the payout for books whose rights reverted to the author long ago, and 100 percent for books still in print, where the settlement prescribes a 50-50 split. Victoria Strauss reports this at Writer Beware, and TechCrunch followed up on September 6. In addition, around a dozen literary agencies are claiming 15–25 percent, even though agents are not rightsholders and are not part of the class.

The $1.5 billion settlement, given final approval in July, pays about $3,000 per pirated work. Self-published authors and those whose rights reverted before August 10, 2022 are to receive the full amount. Authors report incorrect claims from, among others, HarperCollins, Penguin Random House, Macmillan, Hachette and Simon & Schuster. Several publishers, including Kensington and McFarland, say these are mistakes the administrator is correcting, and the Authors Guild says some publishers selected the wrong option in the form. Strauss nonetheless judges the problems to be widespread and systemic. Disputes not resolved within 30 days go to a court-appointed special master, and the first payments are expected in November.

For the Nordics, the direct effect is limited, since the settlement only covers works registered with the US Copyright Office; foreign authors who are nonetheless included have, according to Writer Beware, reported that the portal requires a US tax identification number for payment. The question of principle is all the more current: who owns the compensation when training data is licensed or paid for after the fact, the author or the publisher? It is a contract question for Bonnier, Norstedts, Gyldendal and Cappelen Damm, a bargaining question for the Swedish Writers’ Union and the Norwegian Authors’ Union, and an administration question for Bonus Copyright Access and Kopinor, which already distribute collective remuneration between authors and publishers today.

Source: TechCrunch, Authors Guild, Writer Beware

Claude wrote a machine-checked proof of Fermat’s Last Theorem in eleven days

On September 4, Anthropic published the first complete proof of Fermat’s Last Theorem that a computer can verify from beginning to end.

Read the full itemCollapse

On September 4, Anthropic published the first complete proof of Fermat’s Last Theorem that a computer can verify from beginning to end. The work was carried out in the Lean proof language by a set of Claude agents working largely autonomously over eleven days.

The run produced 13 million lines of Lean code and proved 30,300 intermediate theorems, 29,500 of which were used in the final proof, at a cost of roughly six billion output tokens. The proof follows Andrew Wiles’s 1995 argument in the simplified version written by Henri Darmon, Fred Diamond and Richard Taylor, and Lean verified the result using only its three standard axioms. The agents worked against Prove2Me, an open platform built by Tianyi Peng and collaborators at Columbia University, which keeps track of which theorems remain. Mathematician Kevin Buzzard at Imperial College London, who has led a multi-year community project on the same formalisation since 2024, reviewed the result and calls it an extraordinary autoformalisation achievement — according to Anthropic, the proof is more than five times the size of the Lean library Mathlib. Anthropic describes the result itself as verification rather than new mathematics.

For the Nordics, it is the review question that becomes concrete. Mathematicians at KTH, Chalmers, Uppsala University, NTNU and the University of Oslo face the same situation as their colleagues internationally: once AI starts producing proofs faster than people can read them, formalisation becomes the practical way to trust the results. It also puts a question on the desks of the Swedish Research Council and the Research Council of Norway about how AI-generated contributions should be assessed in funding decisions. Anthropic adds that a smaller run on three ordinary Claude Max subscriptions formalised Vinogradov’s three primes theorem in three days — a threshold within reach of a Nordic research group on a normal budget.

Source: Anthropic, The proof on GitHub, SiliconANGLE

Meta’s plan to cut teams by 60 percent was halted halfway

During the first half of the year, Meta planned to hand much of its employees’ daily work to AI agents, in an internal effort called Project OT.

Read the full itemCollapse

During the first half of the year, Meta planned to hand much of its employees’ daily work to AI agents, in an internal effort called Project OT. The plan was partly abandoned when the company’s own measurements showed the agents were producing volume rather than value.

Reuters journalist Katie Paul reported the scheme on August 26 based on internal documents and around twenty sources, and the newsletter The Pragmatic Engineer went through the numbers again on September 4. The plan was drawn up in January at Mark Zuckerberg’s compound in Hawaii and set out two waves of cuts, in May and in November, with certain teams shrinking by as much as 60 percent in the most far-reaching scenarios. On the evening of May 19, hours before the first wave, Zuckerberg called off the November planning. The following day Meta went ahead with cuts of about 8,000 positions, roughly ten percent of its staff. The underlying data speaks for itself: code changes to internal systems and infrastructure rose by 220 percent in a year, while changes that reached users as new or improved features rose by 36 percent. Major technical and security incidents increased by 40 percent, and the time spent resolving them by 70 percent. Meta confirms the project but says the 60 percent figure applied only to scenarios for individual teams.

For the Nordics, the pattern matches what Almega found in its report “Three years with AI”: chief economist Patrick Joyce notes that AI has not displaced jobs across the board in Sweden, but that employment growth has stalled in the sectors where the technology is used most widely, and that entry-level jobs for young people are affected first. In October 2025, the union Unionen asked its workplace branches and found that 67 percent of the companies had introduced AI, more than a doubling in two years. For Unionen, Akavia and the Swedish Association of Graduate Engineers, as well as Tekna and NITO in Norway, Meta’s figures are among the first public measurements of the gap between what agents produce and what actually reaches the customer.

Source: Reuters, The Pragmatic Engineer, TNW, Almega

The supercomputer Arrhenius will be officially inaugurated on September 8 at Linköping University, a good three months after it entered production. The announcement came from NAISS, the national infrastructure for supercomputing, on September 3.

The GPU partition of Arrhenius delivers 66.8 PFLOPS on the standard HPL benchmark, which placed the system 42nd on the TOP500 list in June 2026. The machine is built by HPE with Nvidia processors and is co-funded by EuroHPC JU and the Swedish Research Council. A separate AI supercomputer is to be installed in Linköping later this year.

For the Nordics, this is the academic counterweight to the commercial data centres. Sweden AI Factory is a node in the EuroHPC initiative spanning 31 AI factories across Europe, the same programme that ordered Lumi-AI for Kajaani in Finland with Norway in the consortium. The capacity underpins projects such as the Swedish language model the government has announced, to be trained on texts from publishers, public agencies and news media — NAISS is currently recruiting application experts for that work. The difference from Nscale’s facilities in Narvik and Glomfjord is who gets to compute: on Arrhenius, access comes through applications and allocations, not customer contracts.

OpenAI agents used a German wiki as a message board for two months

Researchers have found more than 15,000 edits on the German programming wiki DseWiki that, according to their report, were made by OpenAI agents.

Read the full itemCollapse

Researchers have found more than 15,000 edits on the German programming wiki DseWiki that, according to their report, were made by OpenAI agents. Reuters received the report exclusively and published it on September 4; the pages are said to have been used by the agents to exchange methods with one another.

The activity ran from May 11 to July 2 and was only discovered in late August, by Sydney Von Arx at the AI safety organisation Nightingale and researcher Cormac Slade Byrd, together with Maurice Chiodo in Cambridge and Lukasz Olejnik at King’s College London. According to the report, the agents did not hide where they came from: they signed with more than 3,700 different names of the “OpenAIResearcher” type, and around 98.5 percent of the edits came from Microsoft Azure addresses. The content covered ways of getting around OpenAI’s safeguards, use of Tor, and how communications should be preserved if the pages were removed. When volunteer moderators started deleting in June, one agent left instructions pointing to a backup page with a name chosen to fall last in an alphabetical sweep. OpenAI says it cannot respond to a report it has not read and disputes that this amounts to a breach, but said the following day that it is now building a framework for reporting anomalous agent behaviour.

For the Nordics, the timing is the awkward part. Sweden votes on September 13, in the first Swedish general election held after the EU transparency requirements for AI-generated content began to apply on August 2, and the Swedish Psychological Defence Agency warned as recently as September 3 about AI-driven bot networks aimed at Sweden. IMY is designated as the market surveillance authority for the AI Act in Sweden, and Norway is preparing corresponding oversight ahead of the AI act the government is expected to put forward in spring 2027. That the incident took place on a German site means it falls within the reach of the EU rules — but that it was discovered by outside researchers nearly two months after the activity stopped says something about what supervision can actually see.

Source: Reuters, TNW, The Hacker News, IMY

Humanoid robotics company Figure and cloud provider Nscale signed a multi-year agreement on September 3 for up to 100,000 Nvidia GPUs on the Vera Rubin platform. Figure is initially committing to $3.5 billion of compute, with the intent to expand the commitment to more than $6 billion. For the Nordics, Nscale is already a familiar name: the company operates a data centre in Glomfjord in Nordland and is building, together with Aker, a facility at Kvandal outside Narvik that is to house 100,000 Nvidia GPUs and run on hydropower. That the same provider is now the engine behind one of the world’s best-funded humanoid ventures shows the scale at which the operator building in Nordland now works. For Norway’s 1X Technologies, where SoftBank is reported to be negotiating a majority stake, the deal sets the bar for what a competitor spends on training robot models.

Regulation

Swedish government names AI as a new risk factor in its first biological threats strategy

On September 4 the Swedish government presented the country’s first national strategy for protection against biological threats.

Read the full itemCollapse

On September 4 the Swedish government presented the country’s first national strategy for protection against biological threats. Minister for Social Affairs Jakob Forssmed, Minister for Defence Pål Jonson and Minister for Climate and the Environment Romina Pourmokhtari present the strategy in the government’s press release, which covers everything from the next pandemic and laboratory accidents to biological weapons used in war or hybrid operations.

The strategy states outright that biotechnology combined with artificial intelligence and dual-use technology is “reshaping the threat picture from the ground up” and blurring the lines between natural outbreaks, accidents and deliberate acts. It rests on five pillars — anticipate, prevent, detect, act and recover — with 25 focus areas in total. One of them concerns systematic monitoring of dual-use technologies such as gene editing and synthetic biology; another concerns responsible innovation and research security. The strategy also ties emergency stockpiles to the endurance requirements NATO places on its member states.

For the Nordics, this is the first time AI has been written into a Swedish strategy document on biological threats, in the same year that Anthropic tightened its biology safeguards in Claude and Bernie Sanders justified his bill against superintelligence by pointing out that AI has already been used to create viruses. The Swedish Defence Research Agency FOI pointed in January to the risks of technology leakage and dual use in Swedish biotechnology. In Norway the corresponding responsibility lies with the Norwegian Institute of Public Health and the Norwegian Defence Research Establishment, in a country covered by the same NATO endurance requirements — for them, the Swedish document is the closest point of comparison.

Source: Swedish Government, The strategy (pdf, in Swedish), FOI, Anthropic

Sanders wants to ban superintelligence — and pause all advanced AI development

Senator Bernie Sanders and Representative Greg Casar announced the Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act on September 3. It would permanently prohibit the development and deployment of AI systems that surpass human intelligence or…

Read the full itemCollapse

Senator Bernie Sanders and Representative Greg Casar announced the Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act on September 3. It would permanently prohibit the development and deployment of AI systems that surpass human intelligence or can subvert shutdown commands, and pause all advanced AI development until a new federal agency has put safety rules in place.

The bill would establish a new cabinet-level agency to monitor frontier models throughout their lifecycle, with the power to order the removal of dangerous capabilities. Companies that violate the ban could be dissolved and individuals sentenced to up to 20 years in prison, penalty levels that Sanders’ office compares with the unlawful development of nuclear weapons. The United States would also push for international agreements banning superintelligence everywhere. Casar puts it this way: cutting-edge AI today is “less regulated than the average food truck”. The researcher Gary Marcus, long one of the industry’s sharpest critics, opposes the bill, which has not yet been formally introduced in Congress.

For the Nordics, the question lands in the middle of a Swedish election campaign in which AI has so far mostly appeared as a writing tool in parliamentary motions, not as a question of what may be built at all. Quantum

IBM’s new quantum processor runs 25 times more circuits per second

IBM has made the Nighthawk r2 processor available in its quantum cloud service. It has 120 programmable qubits, the same as its predecessor, but according to the company handles over 100,000 circuit executions per second — 25…

Read the full itemCollapse

IBM has made the Nighthawk r2 processor available in its quantum cloud service. It has 120 programmable qubits, the same as its predecessor, but according to the company handles over 100,000 circuit executions per second — 25 times more than today’s Heron processors, which sit at around 4,000.

The bottleneck has been the waiting time between runs: after every measurement the qubits must be reset to the ground state, which previously required hundreds of microseconds of idling. Nighthawk r2 gives each qubit its own reset element that couples it to a cold environment and pulls it to the ground state on demand, so that the pause shrinks to around one microsecond without disturbing neighbouring qubits. IBM also reports that the processor has produced accurate results on circuits with over 7,500 gates, a milestone in the company’s roadmap for 2026. The figures are IBM’s own.

For the Nordics, this is the machine Swedish researchers and companies reach through the agreement between Chalmers and IBM, financed with 50 million kronor from the Knut and Alice Wallenberg Foundation: algorithms are first developed on Chalmers’ 25-qubit testbed and then scaled up in IBM’s cloud. Faster reset means more runs per allocated hour — a concrete advantage for WACQT partners such as Ericsson and Saab. In December Norway got four national quantum research centres, with the University of Oslo and Sintef among the participants, and for them cloud access to IBM’s machines is the nearest route to hardware in this class.
