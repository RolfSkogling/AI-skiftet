In the Foothills of the Singularity

or: a FINRA for AI. Demis Hassabis proposes voluntary pre-release review of frontier models that can be ratcheted up into binding requirements. What the proposal contains, why it comes now — and where the Nordics land as two regulatory philosophies run in parallel.

On July 14, Demis Hassabis — 2024 Nobel laureate in chemistry, CEO and co-founder of Google DeepMind — published the essay “A Framework for Frontier AI and the Dawning of a New Age” on Substack and X. The premise is not modest: AGI is “probably only a few short years away”, we are standing “in the foothills of the singularity”, and the technology’s impact could be “perhaps 10x of the Industrial Revolution at 10x the speed”. But the core of the essay is not the vision — it is a concrete institutional proposal, and it deserves a careful reading.

What Hassabis actually proposes

Hassabis wants the US to establish a standards body for frontier AI, modeled on FINRA — the American financial industry’s self-regulatory organisation, which polices Wall Street under federal oversight. The body would be a public-private partnership, funded mostly by industry, with a board including independent technical experts and open-source representatives.

The mechanics come in three steps. First, the body defines which models are “Frontier-class” through a set of benchmarks updated continuously — perhaps quarterly to start — with saturated tests deprecated and, over time, the body building its own held-out test suites independent of the labs to prevent overfitting. Second, “Frontier Labs” voluntarily share their models with the body for review up to 30 days before release: rigorous scientific evaluations of cyber capabilities, biological threats and other high-risk domains, plus agentic tests looking for attempts to bypass safety guardrails or signs of deception. Once the assessment protocol has proven robust, it can be formalised — passing the tests would then become a requirement for deploying a frontier model in the US market at all.

Then comes what Hassabis calls the framework’s strength: it “could be ratcheted up if the seriousness of the situation demands”, including coordinating a slowdown in development among the Frontier Labs. The rules would apply to frontier models regardless of country of origin and regardless of whether the weights are open or closed, while smaller models from startups and academia are exempt entirely.

Why now

The timing is no accident. In an interview with Axios the same day, Hassabis described today’s AI-driven cyber risks as “warning shots” and estimated that far graver biological and nuclear capabilities could exist in models beyond any government’s control within 18 months. According to Axios, he has spent months building support for the plan with the Trump administration, fellow lab leaders and European officials, and wants the body operational “before year-end”.

The backdrop is the improvised American model politics of this spring. Anthropic’s most powerful models were frozen overnight by an export-control order and negotiated back over two and a half weeks — with no established rules or playbook. OpenAI agreed to restrict GPT-5.6 to government-vetted partners at launch to avoid the same fate. TechCrunch notes, meanwhile, that White House AI advisor Sriram Krishnan has dismissed a classic agency solution — “there will not be an FDA for AI”. Hassabis’ proposal is tailored to that political reality: an industry-funded self-regulatory organisation is the construction that might pass a Washington that rejects new agencies. That the messenger is a Nobel laureate respected across the field’s warring camps — and that Anthropic’s Dario Amodei has in parallel proposed an FAA-style agency, so the frontier lab leaders now mainly dispute who should hold the baton — amplifies the signal.

Strengths and weaknesses

The proposal’s strengths are real. It is technically focused, built to keep pace with the field’s acceleration, and it contains something as unusual as a built-in braking mechanism proposed by a frontier lab chief. The reception among industry peers was positive — Anthropic’s Jack Clark called it “excellent” to see the framework laid out.

The criticism, compiled among others by AI analyst Zvi Mowshowitz, hits three sore points. Voluntariness: the review covers models intended for release, and as researcher Eli Tyre has pointed out, models deployed only internally are thereby exempt — where much of the risk lies, not least automated AI research. Funding: a body paid by those it is meant to scrutinise carries a built-in conflict of interest, and as Peter Wildeford put it, a FINRA needs an SEC above it — self-regulation is a complement to government oversight, not a substitute. And seriousness: MIRI’s Nate Soares noted that Hassabis writes that “experts disagree” without mentioning that the disagreement concerns whether the risk of total catastrophe is around five percent or above fifty — and that the framework lacks a plan for what happens once a model is judged dangerous. Moreover, the proposal is explicitly US-centered: an American body vetting access to the American market, with international standards as an aspiration rather than a mechanism.

For the Nordics

For the Nordics, the most striking feature is that two regulatory philosophies now run in parallel. The EU has already chosen binding law: the AI Act’s transparency requirements have applied since August 2, PTS is proposed as Sweden’s supervisory authority and Nkom takes the coordinating role in Norway. Hassabis proposes the opposite order — start voluntary and technical, formalise once the method is proven. For Swedish and Norwegian companies building on American frontier models, both tracks may end up applying at once: an American body deciding which models reach the market, and a European rulebook deciding how they may be used here. If Hassabis’ framework becomes reality before year-end, the question arises where European — and Nordic — voices come in: the essay names international consensus as a goal, but the board seats he sketches are American. That is as good a reason as any for the Riksdag and the Storting to follow this process as participants, not spectators.
