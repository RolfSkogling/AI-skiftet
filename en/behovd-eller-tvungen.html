Needed or Compelled

or: what actually disappears when economic compulsion ends? Most of what we call being needed is economic compulsion in disguise. The question after work is therefore not how we replace what we had, but whether we ever built it.

Essay · Rolf Skogling · Published August 19, 2026 · approx. 16 min read

Almost nobody works voluntarily. That is neither an accusation nor an outburst, just what you see when you ask: people go to work because the alternative is not getting by. Economic compulsion, in other words. We usually call it something else.

It matters, since being needed is normally counted among the things we stand to lose when human labour stops being necessary. But most of what we call being needed at work is not being needed. If you fail to turn up for a shift, someone can cover — a stand-in, a reshuffle, at worst a delay. What genuinely cannot be replaced is something else: a child, a friend who calls at two in the morning, a team that is one man short, a neighbour who cannot manage if nobody looks in. There is no hire to put in that gap.

The objection sharpens the argument rather than weakening it. If what is lifted away when economic compulsion ends is mostly compulsion, then it is not meaning that disappears. What disappears is an imitation — a form of being needed that resembled the real thing closely enough that we never noticed we lacked it. The danger is therefore not that we lose something we had, but that we discover we never built it.

One qualification straight away, or it will chafe throughout. Being needed is one source of meaning among several. The joy of making things, curiosity, belonging, beauty, love, mastery — none of it disappears when work does. What makes being needed worth an essay is that it is the only one of them that a society without economic compulsion systematically withdraws. That asymmetry is the point; the rest of the list I mention and move past.

What grew when Swedes got time

Between 1992 and 2019 the share of adult Swedes who had done voluntary work in the past year was essentially unchanged: between 48 and 53 per cent, survey after survey.1 Twenty-seven years of rising real incomes and better health — and the curve barely moves.

But something did move in the same data. Informal help — regular support for someone outside your own household, unpaid or for token compensation — rose from 28 to 46 per cent over the same period.1

One curve measures organised activity. The other measures someone being personally dependent on you. When Swedes got more time, more money and better health, it was not the activity that grew. It was the dependence.

The increase should be read with reservations, and there are several. The series is not a smooth rise: it is flat through the 1990s, jumps to 52 per cent in 2005, falls back and rises again towards the end. The report itself notes that part of the jump follows from the question being widened in 2005 to capture companionship, looking in and minding. The population also aged over the period, and the report's own age breakdown shows the sharpest increase in the 75–84 group. How much of the rise is demography, how much is the change in wording and how much is real change cannot be settled from the material. The direction holds nonetheless.

Two kinds of occupation

Meaningful occupations fall into two kinds with entirely different mechanics.

The first lies in the activity itself. You play in order to play, read in order to read, climb the mountain because the mountain is there. The meaning is built into the act and requires no recipient.

The second does not lie in the activity at all, but in the fact that someone else is worse off if you do not do it. The parent. The person sitting with the dying. The fourth crew member of a retained fire unit called out at three in the morning. What the act consists of matters less; what carries it is the dependence.

The difference shows when you remove the recipient. In the first kind: nothing happens. In the second: the activity collapses immediately. Nobody sits in a waiting room for the waiting room's sake.

The distinction can be tested, because we already have a large-scale case in which economic compulsion ends: retirement. If what mattered lay in the activity, the outcome ought to be uniform — everyone gets the same thing, namely time. That is not what the research shows. A meta-analysis of more than half a million individuals finds protection rather than harm, just under 20 per cent lower risk of depression, but with high heterogeneity.2 An older American panel analysis points the other way: a 6–9 per cent deterioration in mental health on full retirement.3 European longitudinal data show a short-term improvement that does not merely fade but reverses: the risk of depression rises again from the tenth year in some groups, and the risk of suicidal ideation is 30 per cent higher after five to nine years and 47 per cent higher after ten years or more.4 Japanese data on 62,438 people show increased depressive symptoms, most clearly among men from lower occupational classes.5

The figures do not point the same way, and that is itself the result: the dispersion is larger than the mean. What explains it is harder to establish than one would wish. Social participation — roles, assignments, settings where someone counts on you — is the factor singled out, but the support is weaker than the argument deserves: in the study that tested the question most directly, the mitigating effect was a non-significant trend, and it appeared only for recreational participation, not for volunteering.5 And the most common role is the most prosaic one: between 24 and 60 per cent of those who are grandparents look after grandchildren.6

The analogy is incomplete, and that should be said plainly. Someone retiring has a working life behind them and a transition that is culturally legitimate in a way that an imposed one never is. The analogy therefore probably overstates how well things go. That makes the conclusion more cautious, not weaker: even under the most favourable conditions we know of, the outcome of freed-up time is a distribution, not a state, and what decides where you land is whether anyone needs you.

The scarcity we choose

If dependence is what carries, people ought to manufacture it where it is absent. They do.

For the Vasaloppet ski race on 1 March 2026, 14,300 people had entered to ski 90 kilometres between Sälen and Mora. Counting the whole of the race week, the figure was more than 61,600. Almost half of those entered for the main race had never skied it before.7 Nobody depends on their doing so. Nothing is produced, nobody is supported, and transport between the two towns was long ago solved by car in under two hours.

What is going on is that the distance is being put back deliberately. You remove the engine, reinstate the stretch and make it hard again. The same mechanism appears in crafts performed more slowly than the machine could manage, in gardens that cost more than the vegetables, in choirs rehearsing for six months for a concert nobody asked for.

I have used the term artificial scarcity before, and always negatively: platforms throttling access to something that is free to copy, subscriptions turning ownership into rental. That is scarcity someone else imposes on you in order to charge for it. Here the same mechanism is turned the right way round.

What separates it from mere enjoyment is that chosen scarcity creates an obligation. A start time to be kept. A handover where someone stands waiting. A team that is one man short. The construction is voluntary; the obligation inside it is not, and it is the obligation that does the work. Chosen scarcity is not play. It is manufactured dependence.

The mechanism is fussy. It works only when the scarcity is real once chosen. The track is real, the cold is real, and whoever has not trained will be left standing at Evertsberg. A constructed necessity whose resistance can be negotiated away on the spot is no necessity. It is a competitive discipline in self-deception.

Being needed cannot be printed

If the argument holds, an uncomfortable problem follows, and it is my own.

Calories can be produced in greater quantity. Dependence cannot. Being needed presupposes someone who lacks what you have. A mentor requires someone who cannot yet do it. A caring assignment requires a care need. An emergency call requires an accident. The better a society becomes at abolishing dependencies — and abolishing dependencies is more or less the definition of welfare and technology — the fewer remain to distribute.

This is a structural constraint, not a question of status. Some roles cannot be given to everyone at once, because they presuppose that someone stands at the opposite end. The patron needs someone to fund, the mentor someone to train. If everyone is a patron, nobody is left to give to. Access to education, healthcare and calories can be expanded; access to someone needing you specifically cannot.

Here lies the labour market's most underrated function. Wage labour was a poor distribution mechanism for money and a strangely effective distribution mechanism for dependence. It placed millions of people daily in situations where someone else depended on their turning up. That most of this was compulsion rather than genuine need does not make it insignificant — the compulsion produced real being-needed as a by-product, on a scale nothing else we have built comes close to.

That is the function that disappears first, long before the wage does.

Those who never get to start

I have written before about what happens when the development ladder is pulled up for entire countries: the industrial route and the services route can vanish simultaneously for economies that still need them.8 I will not repeat that analysis. But the movement has an individual counterpart that I left untreated.

Anyone who never enters working life never gets even the counterfeit version of being needed. That sounds like a small loss, given that the version is counterfeit. It is not. Counterfeit being-needed comes with a standing — a shift, a colleague who counts on you, a place where your absence is noticed — and it is out of that standing that genuine dependencies tend to grow. You become the one people ask for help, you end up on the association's board, you acquire a reputation for being reliable. In practice, the route to the genuine has run through the counterfeit.

The research shows narrower things than it is usually used for. That early exclusion is costly is well established: Swedish vocational upper-secondary graduates who were unemployed straight after finishing had roughly 17 per cent lower annual income five years later than their siblings.9 Belgian school-leavers still unemployed after nine months had dramatically worse odds of getting in at all — if entry was delayed by a further year, the probability of finding work within two years fell from 60 to 16 per cent for men.10 But the literature is not unambiguous in the direction it is usually cited for. The most comprehensive review finds that the income effects of entering during a recession fade after ten to fifteen years — while at the same time reporting lasting effects on family formation, health and mid-life mortality, and an earnings gap that reopens at precisely that age.11 And the study most often invoked as proof of permanent scarring in fact draws the opposite conclusion about employment.12 The picture, then, is not that the scars are smaller than assumed. It is that they sit somewhere other than where the literature tends to look.

Closest to my argument lies not the income research but the health research. Everyone who finished the final year of compulsory school in a medium-sized industrial town in northern Sweden in 1981 — Luleå — was followed for twenty-seven years, with mental health measured already at sixteen, that is, before exposure. Open youth unemployment was associated with an elevated risk of mental health problems at twenty-one and still at forty-three: odds ratios of 2.48 and 1.71 respectively. Labour market programmes were not — 0.95 at twenty-one and 1.23 at forty-three, the latter not statistically significant.13

The difference between the two is not the income. It is that someone expected you to turn up.

I do not conclude that it was being needed that did the work. The programmes also provided structure, activity and belonging, and the study cannot separate the components. The authors themselves write that the programmes leave "no or minimal scars" — not none at all; in the unadjusted model, programme participation was in fact linked to worse health at forty-three. The cohort is moreover a single town, at a time when adult unemployment stood at around three per cent. But it is the most telling contrast I have found, and it points the same way as everything else in this essay.

A separate analysis of the same cohort shows the asymmetry across the life course: youth unemployment is associated with worse mental health at 21, 30 and 42, while unemployment later in life produces problems at the time but not lasting ones.14 That is exactly what one would expect if what is damaged is entry into a role rather than an income. But the studies are conducted in a single industrial town and cannot separate cause from selection.

The present situation calls for caution. Youth unemployment in Sweden averaged 24.3 per cent over 2025 — a figure that looks worse than it is. Unemployment is measured as a share of the labour force, and most people in that age group study full time without seeking work and are therefore not counted in the labour force at all. Full-time students who do seek work, on the other hand, are counted as unemployed: 102,000 of the 170,000 unemployed young people were full-time students.15 In American payroll data, employment among 22–25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed occupations is 19 per cent below the level it would have reached had it tracked peers in less exposed occupations, and the mechanism is absent hiring rather than dismissal. The authors state explicitly that they do not show AI caused it.16

Established, then: early exclusion costs in income, and it costs in mental health in a way that later unemployment does not. Unestablished — my own inference — is that the latter cost arises because what is missing is not a wage but an entry.

Cultural asymmetry

If what has to be replaced is a distribution mechanism for dependence, the shift lands with different force depending on where in a society the chains of dependence sit.

Societies that never located human worth in productivity have less to lose. Where standing derives from lineage, age, religious position or family, the chains of dependence already sit outside the labour market and keep working as it thins out. That is not an argument that such arrangements are better, but a statement about what they are exposed to.

The secular, individualistic achievement cultures are the most exposed, and Sweden is among them. The Swedish work-first doctrine is not an administrative arrangement but a moral idea with deep roots in Lutheran northern Europe: whoever works contributes, whoever draws a wage has earned it, whoever stands outside becomes not only economically but symbolically weaker.17 Such an idea does not disappear when work does. It becomes homeless, looks for something to attach to, and readily attaches to the wrong things.

It is the same kind of inversion that applies demographically: where the workforce is shrinking, technology solves a shortage; where it is growing, technology removes the entry-level jobs. The risk is therefore greatest where the need for labour is greatest.8 The more tightly a society has tied human worth to productivity, the greater the loss when productivity becomes free.

The closest real cases are the rentier economies of the Gulf, where material scarcity is largely alleviated by resource income. Around three-quarters of employed Kuwaitis were working in the government sector at the end of 2025.18 What that has done to the experience of meaning I do not know, and will not pretend otherwise. What I can say is weaker: where the work requirement was lifted, no new way of being needed was built, but public employment that the IMF describes as oversized, costly and a drag on productivity growth in the economy as a whole. Jobs were manufactured instead of dependencies. A warning sign, not a proof.

Where it hurts

Two points about the sequence in time, both offered as premises rather than investigations.

The first: the distribution of material abundance is a political question, not a technical one. The world's supply of dietary energy passed 3,000 kilocalories per person per day in 2023 — 3,016 kilocalories, far above average global energy requirements, albeit measured as availability at the retail level rather than actual intake19 — and yet between 638 and 720 million people were undernourished in 2024.20 Abundance did not abolish scarcity, because hunger was never a production problem.21

The second: the crisis comes in the transition, not at the destination. The cognition curve and the atom curve are diverging — the cognition-heavy accelerates while robotics, power grids and construction lag.22 Anthropic's August risk report supports the picture, with the limits of its method openly stated. Section 3.6 rests on semi-structured interviews with 31 experts, described by Anthropic themselves as "a rough litmus test, rather than a rigorous or comprehensive assessment". One of the five energy experts interviewed emphasised that "physical deployment (e.g. building transmission infrastructure), not R&D, is a significant and not-yet automated constraint". At the same time, Anthropic writes in its own name that it is plausible that automated research and development will become "a major concern in the next 6–12 months".23

Individual judgements, not measurements. But read together they give the transition a shape: cognition may run ahead while material abundance queues for transformers and permits. An occupation can lose its value long before prosperity reaches the person who practised it. It is in that gap that the question bites: what am I when what I could do is no longer in demand, and the money has not yet arrived?

What is weak in this

Four objections to my own argument, in descending order of how much they trouble me.

The mixing problem. Compulsion and genuine being-needed cannot be cleanly separated, and the entire essay presupposes that they can. The nurse works for the wage and is genuinely needed, simultaneously, in the same act. The shift is economically imposed and the patient genuinely dependent. That I can point to pure cases at either end does not mean the middle is empty; the middle is probably where most people are. Worse: compulsion has produced a great deal of real being-needed as a by-product. It forced people into rooms where others actually needed them. That this being-needed will arise by itself once the compulsion is removed is an assumption, and I have no support for it.

The transparency problem. Manufactured necessity works only if you can forget it was manufactured. The ski track carries because the cold and the body are real. A civic assignment that everyone knows is arranged for the participant's sake risks feeling like therapy. I see no obvious route from "we ought to construct necessity" to "this feels necessary", and that is precisely the route that would be needed.

The availability problem. Voluntary scarcity is already available to everyone — associations, choral singing, crafts, retained fire services, endurance sport — and yet the share who take part in organised form has stood still for twenty-seven years, even as both time and money increased.1 If self-chosen necessity were the solution, we ought to have seen it grow with prosperity. The honest reading is that what grew instead was informal help, that is, dependence itself. But I cannot rule out the simpler explanation: that most people simply do not want to.

Administrability. Distributing goods is routine for a state. How to write the distribution of being needed into a budget bill I do not know. The institutions I have proposed before — learning centres, civic assignments, support for care work24 — distribute opportunities, not dependence. Nobody becomes needed by being assigned a task.

Freeing up is never the point

Let me close with what I know best.

An organisation that frees up capacity without having decided what it is to be directed towards decays. It is the most predictable thing I know of in improvement work. You remove waste, streamline a line, get time back — and if nobody has decided what the time is for, it fills up with something worse than what was removed. Freeing up is never the point. Redirection is.

I see no reason to believe a society behaves better than a workshop. And the redirection required is not primarily about money or time. It is about dependence: building settings where it actually matters whether a particular person turns up or not.

Here the objection I began with lands. If almost nobody works voluntarily, then it is not meaning that is lifted away when economic compulsion is lifted away. It is the imitation — the form of being needed that resembled the real thing closely enough that we never noticed we lacked it. The danger is therefore not that we lose something we had. It is that we discover we never built it.

The question after work is therefore not what we shall do with the freedom. It is whether there is anyone who notices if we do not turn up.
